Silas: Eggbert did not begin as a Christmas character. He was created by Cornell University in the early nineteen fifties to promote New York eggs. That was his original assignment. Not spreading holiday cheer. Not greeting generations of families in a nursery. Not becoming the subject of local nostalgia. Selling the idea of eggs. There is something admirable about the simplicity of that mission. A university wanted people to appreciate the state's poultry industry, and somebody decided the solution was a large talking egg. Not a chicken. The egg itself.

Silas: It is difficult to imagine the meeting where this was approved. Presumably, someone proposed an educational display. Someone else suggested making it interactive. And eventually the conversation arrived at the conclusion that the best representative of New York agriculture would be an enormous egg capable of speaking to strangers. At that point, the sensible thing would have been to stop. Instead, they built him. Eggbert made his debut at the nineteen fifty-three New York State Fair. And his introduction was not modest. Governor Thomas Dewey crowned him. Think about that for a moment. A governor of New York participated in the ceremonial elevation of an egg. Not an elected egg, obviously. There is no evidence Eggbert ever sought public office. But he received a crown, which is more than most candidates can say.

Silas: Eggbert worked alongside Cornell's Doctor Bob Baker, promoting the state's poultry industry. His purpose was agricultural education and publicity. The wooden throne and the animated personality gave that purpose an unusual theatrical quality. Eggbert was a promotional character, but one with a certain amount of institutional dignity. He had Cornell behind him. He had a governor's attention. And he had a job. For a talking egg in the middle of the twentieth century, that is a respectable beginning.

Silas: The next chapter took him away from the state fair and toward the Hudson Valley. In nineteen fifty-one, Clem Devitt founded Devitt's Nursery on Route thirty-two in New Windsor, Orange County. A nursery is a place where people expect plants, landscaping supplies, seasonal decorations, and perhaps the occasional ambitious garden ornament. It is not necessarily where they expect to encounter a large egg with conversational abilities. But Clem's son, Jack Devitt, saw an opportunity. In nineteen seventy-one, Jack bought Eggbert. And he made the character the centerpiece of Christmas on the Farm. That decision changed Eggbert's identity. A figure originally designed to promote New York eggs became a Christmas attraction.

Silas: There was no obvious connection between poultry marketing and the holiday season. Christmas already had an established collection of characters. Santa Claus. Reindeer. Elves. Snowmen. The occasional angel. None of them needed to explain why they were there. Eggbert did. Yet somehow, the explanation became unnecessary. Families came to see him. Children encountered him as part of their Christmas experience. And the large talking egg became a local tradition.

Silas: There is a particular kind of regional affection reserved for things that make very little sense outside the place where they exist. A visitor might ask why a nursery has a talking egg at Christmas. A local resident might simply reply that it always has. That answer is not historically precise. But it captures how traditions become traditions. Something unusual happens once. Then it happens again. Then children grow up remembering it. Eventually, the question is no longer why the egg is there. The question is why the egg would ever be absent.

Silas: Jack Devitt's decision gave Eggbert a second purpose, one that outlasted his original agricultural assignment. The egg was no longer primarily explaining the value of New York poultry. He was becoming part of the area's seasonal memory. And seasonal memory can be surprisingly durable. People remember the decorations they saw as children. They remember the places their parents took them. They remember the strange objects that appeared every December and seemed to belong to the world as naturally as the weather. Eggbert became one of those objects. The fact that he was a giant egg did not weaken the tradition. If anything, it made him more memorable. There are many Christmas attractions. There are considerably fewer talking Christmas eggs.

Silas: For decades, Eggbert occupied his place at Devitt's. But local traditions are not protected from changes in business ownership. In nineteen ninety-six, Devitt sold the nursery to Agway. The location closed in two thousand. Eggbert's last appearance before that closure had been Christmas nineteen ninety-nine. And then he disappeared from public view. For roughly a decade, Eggbert remained in storage.

Silas: This is the point in the story where the tone changes. Because when a beloved local character disappears, people begin filling the silence with explanations. Some are practical. Perhaps the attraction was too expensive to maintain. Perhaps the property changed hands. Perhaps the character was simply packed away. Others are more dramatic. Rumors circulated that Eggbert had been destroyed. Reporting has connected those rumors to a fire at Devitt's. The rumors were wrong. Eggbert had not been destroyed. But the fact that people believed he might have been says something about his place in the community.

Silas: Nobody needs a rumor about the destruction of an ordinary piece of retail equipment. People do not generally mourn a discontinued display rack. Eggbert was different. He had become a character with a history, and his absence created a mystery. For children who remembered visiting him, the disappearance may have felt like the end of a particular version of Christmas. For adults, it was another reminder that familiar places do not necessarily remain familiar forever. Businesses close. Properties change. Objects disappear into storage. And the things people assumed would always be there turn out to have depended on ordinary decisions about money, maintenance, and ownership. Eggbert's story could have ended there. A once-famous promotional egg, preserved somewhere out of sight, remembered mainly by people who had encountered him years earlier. But the Hudson Valley was not finished with him.

Silas: New owners brought Eggbert back in twenty eleven. He was refurbished in twenty twelve. And the comeback gradually became a story of its own. By twenty fifteen, Eggbert was drawing approximately ten thousand visitors. The New York Times covered his return. That is a remarkable journey for a character whose original purpose was promoting eggs. He had gone from a university agricultural campaign to a state fair attraction, then to a nursery Christmas tradition, then into storage, and finally back into public life. The resurrection gave him something that many seasonal characters never acquire. A history of loss and return. People were not simply visiting an unusual holiday display. They were revisiting something they thought might be gone. And there is a difference.

Silas: An attraction can entertain people because it is new. A returning attraction can move people because it is familiar. Eggbert managed to become both. For some visitors, he was a discovery. For others, he was a recovered piece of childhood. That combination helped make the comeback meaningful. It also left one question unanswered. Why an egg? Joe Gizzarelli, a co-owner of Devitt's who has worked there for decades, has acknowledged that he is not entirely certain about the reasoning behind an oversized egg becoming a Christmas icon.

Silas: That is a refreshingly reasonable position. It would be easy to invent an elaborate explanation. One could claim the egg symbolizes renewal, family, the cycle of seasons, or the promise of new beginnings. Those interpretations might sound impressive. But they are not necessary to explain what happened. Jack Devitt bought an unusual character and made him part of a Christmas attraction. People liked him. The tradition continued. Sometimes the simplest explanation is the one that survives contact with the evidence. Eggbert does not need a hidden mythology to justify his existence. His history is strange enough. Silas: He is not a polished character invented by a modern entertainment company to fit an established holiday narrative. He is an old agricultural promotional device that found a second home.
