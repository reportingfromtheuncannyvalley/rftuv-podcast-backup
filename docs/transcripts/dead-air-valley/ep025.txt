Dead Air Valley: "The Town Was the Set"

In the week of October 19, 2026, the city of Beacon, in Dutchess County, had its Halloween early. The decorations were up on Main Street, more dramatically than you would expect for mid-October. A horse-drawn carriage came down the street with the cast of a Netflix television show on board. And the man without a head drove.

That is where we start today. We are back in Dutchess County after yesterday's stop in Carmel, in Putnam County, and before that Poughquag on the 26th, Saugerties on the 25th, Ossining on the 24th, and Peekskill on the 23rd. Dutchess is the home county. It turns out that while we were away, something unusual happened on a main street.

The surface

If you had walked down Main Street in Beacon in the third week of October, you might have glanced at the Halloween decorations and kept walking. It is late October. Towns decorate for Halloween. The pumpkins, the cobwebs, the orange and black bunting, whatever a given town reaches for in the fall, these are unremarkable when the calendar reads the way it read that week.

Except, according to Hudson Valley Country, the decor seemed more dramatic than normal for the time of year. That is a small detail and it does the important work of the whole story. More dramatic than normal. Not wildly out of place. Not impossible to explain away as enthusiasm. Just a degree or two past what you would ordinarily see, in a way that might make you look again, and then decide you were probably imagining it, and keep walking.

Most people who walked that street probably did keep walking. The time of year made the decorations legible. The context made them invisible. A Halloween town is what a main street in late October is supposed to look like, which is precisely why it worked as a television set.

The real Halloween, Saturday the 31st of October, was still ahead when the decorations went up. The actual trick-or-treaters had not arrived. Children in costume had not yet walked that street for the reason children in costume walk streets. The candy was still in the bowl, or in the bag at the grocery store, unmoved. Beacon had the set in the week of October 19. The holiday itself had not come yet.

The ride

Then the carriage came down Main Street.

A horse-drawn carriage carrying the cast of The Four Seasons, with the Headless Horseman at the front. A filmed performer, in character, without a head, on a Hudson Valley main street, pulling a carriage full of television actors.

A Headless Horseman in the Hudson Valley reads immediately. The image belongs to Washington Irving's "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow," published in 1820, a story set in this region, which means a Horseman on a Hudson Valley road arrives pre-loaded with literary context before anyone has to explain a thing. Irving did not write about Beacon, and no local Beacon legend is attached to the figure. But the image travels across the Valley because the story is the Valley's story, and anyone who grew up here or has spent time in this part of New York recognizes it on sight. A production choosing a Headless Horseman for a Hudson Valley Halloween scene is not reaching for a generic symbol. It is choosing the specific one that already belongs here. That is a production choice with some understanding of place behind it.

This Horseman was a performer. That is stated plainly because it is the accurate description. Not a supernatural event, not a local legend brought to life, not anything that requires lowered lights or a spooky voice. A performer, in costume, filming a scene, on a main street in Beacon, in the week of October 19, 2026.

Hudson Valley Country reported the ride, with the cast of The Four Seasons on board the carriage. An Instagram account, beastiebunny, caught the cast taking a hayride down Main Street, per that same account. And a Beacon local publication, A Little Beacon Blog, published its own video on October 20, 2026, with the title "Headless Horseman Down Main Street Beacon For Filming of The Four Seasons." That is two independent local sources, one written and one video, placing the same scene on the same street in the same week. The ride is well-documented.

The show

The Four Seasons is a Netflix show. Season 2 had already wrapped by the time Central Casting came calling. Netflix announced season 3. In September, according to 94.3 Lite FM, the cast was confirmed back on set for season 3, now in production. The Hudson Valley had hosted the show before and was on the list again.

Hudson Valley Country's account of the Beacon filming says the production must be working on a Halloween episode for season 3. That "must be" is Hudson Valley Country's inference, drawn from the Halloween-themed staging on Main Street and the presence of the cast. It is not a Netflix statement. No Netflix confirmation of a Halloween episode appears in any source used for this episode. No episode air date was given in any source used here. What Hudson Valley Country observed was a production decision that points in a particular direction. Where that episode lands in the season, when it airs, what exactly it contains, none of that is established in the sources for today.

What is established is that a Netflix production was in Beacon, that it dressed a main street for Halloween, and that the Headless Horseman rode down that street for the camera. The inference about the episode is reasonable. It is just an inference.

The locals as background

After season 2 wrapped, Central Casting posted a call that said this: "We are now casting locals in the Newburgh and Beacon areas for the hit Netflix show The Four Seasons! See the details on how to submit if you live there."

That call, carried by both Hudson Valley Country and 94.3 Lite FM, sought locals of all ages, genders, and ethnicities as paid background actors for interior and exterior scenes. No filming dates were given in the call. It was an open submission for people who already lived in those two Hudson Valley areas.

This is worth sitting with for a moment. The production was not just renting a location and filling it with actors and crew. It was also asking the residents of the location to apply to stand in it. Not to play a character in a fictional place. To be themselves, or a version of themselves in a Halloween costume, on a street they already lived near, in a town they already knew. Paid background. Their own main street, on the call sheet.

A television production that films on a real main street benefits from real people in the background. Hired extras from outside a place can look like hired extras from outside a place. A local face in the background of a Beacon main street carries something different, a kind of unconstructed familiarity with the space, the way people actually stand and move in a place they know without thinking about it. Central Casting's call made the logic of this explicit. Beacon residents were not incidental to the set. They were an ingredient in what made the set convincing.

What it means to receive a casting call for your own street is a question the sources do not answer. Whether the residents who submitted felt flattered, or strange about it, or simply thought "that sounds like a good Tuesday," none of that is in any source used here, and this episode will not invent it. What the sources establish is the structure of the offer: the street you live on needs you in the background, it pays, and if you want in, submit through Central Casting.

That is a particular relationship between a place and a production. It is not unusual in the industry. It is worth naming plainly in Beacon, because Beacon is the place where it happened, in the week we are talking about.

There is also something worth examining in the physical experience of that work, though no source describes it for the people who submitted in Beacon. Background work on a location you already know requires a particular kind of performance that is not quite acting and not quite being yourself. You know where the good light falls in the afternoon. You know which end of the block is louder on a weekday. You know the smell of the place in October. None of that knowledge is useful to the production in any direct way, and none of it goes into the frame. What the production wants is your body in the background, moving at a pace that reads as ordinary. The familiarity you carry is working underneath the visible thing, shaping how you hold yourself in a space you did not need to be told how to inhabit. Whether that makes the work easier or stranger is a question the sources do not settle. It is possible that knowing a street too well makes it harder to walk down it as though you are just walking down it, because you are always, at some level, also remembering that you already know it.

The second night

The week of the carriage ride passed. October rolled on. And then, on Tuesday, October 27, Main Street in Beacon was a film set again.

The City of Beacon hosted a film production on Tuesday the 27th through the morning of Wednesday the 28th. The production occupied Main Street between Fishkill and Teller Avenues and East Main Street. The city posted signage for parking restrictions along that stretch. Sidewalks were closed from four in the afternoon until five in the morning of Wednesday. City of Beacon Police were on site to assist with rerouting traffic. By five in the morning, the city statement says, everything was back to normal.

The city statement, as quoted by Hudson Valley Country, does not name the production. The City of Beacon hosted a film production. That is what the official record says. Hudson Valley Country's reading, based on the cast having been spotted throughout town in the preceding weeks and the carriage ride the week before, is that the production was The Four Seasons. That is worth being clear about: the connection between the October 27 closure and any specific show is Hudson Valley Country's inference, not a city confirmation. Anyone stating that the October 27 overnight closure was definitively The Four Seasons is going beyond what the official statement says. The city named no production.

What the official statement does establish is the operational shape of what that night required. Thirteen hours of sidewalk access, from four in the afternoon to five in the morning. Police managing traffic on the reroutes. Parking restriction signage. A deadline for restoration, meaning the production was logistically required to have the street looking like a regular Wednesday morning street before most of Beacon was awake to see anything other than a regular Wednesday morning street.

Think about what that deadline does. The overnight closure is invisible to anyone who went to sleep on a Tuesday and woke up on a Wednesday. The street is a main street again by the time the commuters arrive and the town is awake. The sidewalks are back. The police are gone. The signage is gone. Whatever was filmed in those thirteen hours is in the production's hands. The town resumes.

That restoration deadline is not just logistics. It is the last instruction in the direction to the street: be yourself again before anyone notices you were something else.

A filming night that ends before the town is awake leaves a particular kind of gap in a place's ordinary account of itself. Nobody witnessed the closure who was not there for it. The people who drove past the reroutes may not have known what was being filmed or even that filming was happening. The people who slept through it have no memory of it at all, because they have no experience of it. What remains is not a shared memory of something the town saw but a shared fact about something that happened while most of the town was not looking. The street absorbed a production and returned the street. That is a different kind of event from one that leaves a mark anyone can point to. The mark here is in the footage, which is somewhere else now.

The real one is still three days out

Here is where the story sits today.

Main Street in Beacon is back to normal. The sidewalks reopened on schedule. Whatever was filmed on the night of the 27th into the early morning of the 28th is in a hard drive or a server farm or wherever the current infrastructure of a Netflix production stores what it shoots. The staged Halloween, the one with the carriage and the Headless Horseman and the actors and the locals who may have submitted through Central Casting to stand in their own street as paid background, is being assembled into an episode that nobody has an air date for.

And the real Halloween is still three days away.

Saturday, October 31st, Beacon's own children will do what children do on Halloween. They will go out in costume. They will walk whatever route their Halloween takes them on, the same streets or streets nearby. They will knock on doors. They will collect candy from their neighbors, the same neighbors who, some of them, may have submitted to Central Casting earlier this month. They will do all of it for free, because it is Halloween, because that is what the day is for, because no one from a production company offered them a call and a rate.

The street they walk will be the same street. The decorations, if any remain from what the production put up or what the town added in the days since, will be decorations on a main street that has already had its Halloween once, for a camera, for what Hudson Valley Country believes is a season three episode of a Netflix show about the four seasons, recorded before the actual season, the autumn one, reached the actual holiday it was staging.

A Headless Horseman has already ridden Main Street in Beacon this October. He rode it for a production. He rode it on schedule and in costume. The cast was on board. The hayride was captured on a phone by someone named beastiebunny and by a local blog on video. The town dressed as a Halloween town and the camera recorded the town dressed as a Halloween town.

Saturday, the town will just be a Halloween town. No camera required. The children will not be background. The candy will not be a prop. The costumes will belong to the people wearing them rather than to a costume department. The Horseman will not ride, because the Horseman is a performer and the performer is somewhere else.

Beacon dressed as a Halloween town for a show about the seasons. Then it went back to being Beacon. In three days, it gets to be a Halloween town for real.
