White Plains spent the first half of this week the way the whole county did, watching ballots. You know that part of the story. The machines stumbled on Tuesday, the emergency ballots went into bags, and the counting rolled on into Wednesday out in Ardsley. It was the loudest story in Westchester, and it deserved to be. But on Monday, the day before any of it, the White Plains Department of Public Safety posted a quieter warning on its Facebook page, about a different kind of visitor entirely. Not a voter. Not an official. A film crew. Or rather, and this is the whole story in one phrase, people saying they were a film crew.

Here is how it arrives, according to the reports the department says it has received. Someone contacts a resident or a business owner in White Plains, at home or at a storefront, with wonderful news. A production is coming to town. The production has been looking at properties, and theirs, theirs is exactly right. The house has the look. The shop has the character. Would they be interested in letting the production use the place as a filming location? It is the kind of compliment that does not feel like a sales pitch, because it is aimed at the one thing a person cannot be objective about, which is the building they live or work in. And at first, nothing about it asks for anything except enthusiasm.

I am Silas Van Wyck, and this is Dead Air Valley, where we keep an eye on the ordinary surface of things in these valleys, and on whatever is moving underneath it. Tonight the thing underneath is a production that does not exist, scouting locations in a city that real productions genuinely love. Stay with me, because the difference between the real knock and the fake one turns out to be paperwork, and paperwork, in White Plains, is a subject with a long and peculiar history.

The department of public safety says the approach turns, as these approaches always do, at the moment money enters the conversation. The scammers, and that is the department's finding, not my embroidery, ask for personal banking information. The stated reason is the friendliest one imaginable: they need somewhere to send the payment. The location fee, you understand, has to reach you somehow. In other tellings, the department says, the contact offers something even shinier than a location fee. It offers a paid role in the production. A part. For the homeowner who does not merely own the right house but might, at long last, be discovered standing in it. What the department says these contacts have in common is the machinery underneath the charm. Fake phone numbers. Fake employee names. And, boldest of all, a false claim to represent the City of White Plains itself.

The department's own words are worth hearing plainly, because police departments do not usually get to be the wittiest voice in a story, and this one earns it. Quote: Remember, if an offer sounds too good to be true, it probably is. And then the sentence that is the entire episode in miniature: Never share sensitive banking or personal information with unverified contacts. News 12 carried the warning on Tuesday, while the county was voting. The Journal News carried it today. A scam that begins as a whisper to a few homeowners is now in every paper and on every station in the valley, which is either the end of it or the start of it moving somewhere else. These things travel. That is part of why we are spending the evening on it.

Now, the first question a reasonable person asks is why anyone would believe this. Film crews do not, in the ordinary course of life, wander residential streets offering money for houses. Except that in White Plains, they do. That is not a figure of speech. That is the record.

In February of last year, Steven Spielberg brought a production to White Plains, a science fiction picture called Disclosure Day, with Emily Blunt in front of the cameras. The city closed Hamilton Avenue, between North Broadway and Doctor Martin Luther King Junior Boulevard, for the shoot. Notices went up on the very same Public Safety Facebook page that carried this week's warning, and signs went up on Main Street telling drivers what was coming. A library over in Greenburgh was dressed up as a hospital, complete with rebranded ambulances. The production filmed at Renaissance Plaza, used a loading dock at The Westchester mall, and kept its base camp at a country club in Greenburgh. By the city clerk's count, more than two hundred crew members worked on the picture. The location manager, a woman named Kat Delaney, explained the choice afterward to the county's tourism and film office, and her explanation deserves to be read into the record of this episode exactly as she gave it. White Plains, she said, has the infrastructure and experience to be able to do that and provide that for a film crew. We did not hesitate. We did not worry.

And it was not a first visit. Spielberg had chosen White Plains nearly a decade earlier, for The Post. The same location manager had relied on Westchester for scenes in The Irishman. So when somebody tells a White Plains homeowner that a production wants their house, the homeowner is not believing in magic. They are believing in the recent, documented, highly visible history of their own city. The scam does not invent a fantasy. It rents one, from the truth, at no charge. That is what makes it a Dead Air Valley story and not just a police blotter line. The bizarre thing here is not that the offer is strange. The bizarre thing is how completely normal it sounds, on those streets, in that city, this year.

Because here is the part the scammers are counting on you not knowing. A real film shoot in White Plains is not a handshake and a compliment. It is a small avalanche of paper, and the paper starts moving long before the first truck parks anywhere.

The city has a film production permit application, and productions apply for it through the city in the ordinary way, the way you apply for anything in a city that runs on forms. Before filming, the production distributes film notifications to the property owners and tenants living within five hundred feet of the filming area, on both sides of the street. Think about that distance for a moment. Five hundred feet of neighbors, in both directions, get told in advance that a production is coming, what it is, and when. Written permission is required from owners and tenants whose property is affected by the filming, or by equipment and vehicles parking in front of it, and the city keeps a form for exactly those signatures. Signatures of minors will not do. Signatures of hired staff will not do. The notice a production sends around tells the street, in writing, that the company is applying for a city film permit, that it will abide by the city's filming rules if the permit is granted, and that it will maintain the legally required liability insurance. The notice even invites the street to write back with any special concerns, and gives residents five business days to raise them. Sit with that for a second. A genuine production does not fear the neighborhood's questions. It schedules them, in advance, on a form. A real shoot, in other words, is a public event before it is a private arrangement. By the time a genuine crew wants your house, half your street has already been told, your city clerk's office knows the production's name, and there is a permit application with your address's neighborhood written all over it.

Now hold that picture up against the offer the department is warning about, and the seams show at once. The fake approach is quick where the real one is slow. It is private where the real one is announced to five hundred feet of neighbors. It flatters you personally, you and your house and your undiscovered talent, where the real one mostly wants to know about parking. And it moves, very early, to the one piece of information that no legitimate location arrangement needs before there is a permit, a contract, and a production office you can telephone: your bank account details. A real production needs your house for a few days, and it is prepared to prove, on paper, in advance, to the entire street, that it is who it says it is. The fake production needs your banking information, and it needs it before anything has been proved at all. The house is the excuse. The account is the errand.

The second hook deserves its own moment, because it is easy to laugh at and wrong to. The offer of a paid role. A part in the picture. Consider who that hook is set for. Not for the homeowner with the perfect colonial, who is already flattered by the location fee. It is set for everybody else the contact can reach, the storefront owner, the neighbor, the person whose building was not chosen but whose hopes are portable. A location fee flatters your property. A role flatters you. Between the two of them, the approach has something for nearly everyone it touches, and the price of admission in both cases is the same small, reasonable sounding thing. Your details. So we can process the payment. It is worth noticing how the word payment does the heavy lifting. Nobody is asking you for money. That is the elegance of it, if a thing like this can be called elegant. They are offering you money, and the only obstacle between you and it is a form that needs filling in.

The department's reports, notice, were not only from homeowners. Business owners were contacted too, about storefronts, and that half of the warning should not slide past. A shopkeeper hears the approach differently. A location fee sounds like a good week. A film crew outside sounds like customers. And a business, unlike a household, is accustomed to sending and receiving its banking details in the ordinary course of trade, which makes the ask feel smaller, more routine, more like paperwork and less like a stranger reaching for the till. The approach is fitted to its target, house by house, shop by shop. That is not a compliment to its authors. It is a description of their craft, and the craft is worth seeing clearly.

There is one more layer to this, and it is the layer this program exists to notice. Think about what the prop is. Not a car, which can be driven away and checked. Not a piece of jewelry, which fits in a pocket and can be appraised. A house. The least movable object in a person's life, the one thing that cannot be taken anywhere, hidden anywhere, or mistaken for anything else. The scammers are not really renting houses. They are renting the credibility of a house. A home address is the most verified fact about any of us. It is where the tax bills go. It is where we have been findable, in some cases, for decades. When an offer attaches itself to your house, it borrows all of that permanence, and for a moment the offer feels as solid as the building. But of course the building is the one thing the scammers have no use for whatsoever. They will never park a truck in front of it. They will never need the light in the kitchen at four in the afternoon. The house could be anywhere, could be anything, could frankly be a photograph, and the approach would work exactly as well, because the approach was never about the house. It was about the person who answers for the house, and the account that person keeps.

And spare a thought for the timing, because timing in these matters is rarely an accident even when it is only an opportunity. This approach was being reported in the very days when Westchester County's attention, every bit of it, was pointed at the election. At the machines, at the emergency ballots, at the count. Public safety departments post their warnings when they get their reports, and the department posted this one on Monday, before the voting even began. But a warning competes for the same attention as everything else, and this week the competition was an election that would not sit still. It is entirely possible to live in White Plains, to have followed your city's news faithfully all week, to know the ballot story down to the hour the scanning started in Ardsley, and to have missed entirely the small post about the film crew that is not a film crew. If that is you, then this episode has done its one job, and here is the practical part of it, stated once, the way we do it here.

If you are approached, at home or at your business, by anyone offering to use your property as a filming location, or offering you a paid role in a production, the Department of Public Safety's advice is this. Do not share sensitive banking or personal information with unverified contacts. Before you agree to anything, verify the thing the way the city itself suggests: contact the White Plains city clerk, or write to the city's film office at Filmwp. That is spelled F, I, L, M, W, P, at whiteplainsny.gov. A genuine production will survive that telephone call. It will, in fact, be faintly relieved by it, because genuine productions live inside that paperwork the way fish live in water. The details, and the department's post itself, are in tonight's show notes. If the offer cannot survive one call to the clerk, then you have learned everything the call was for.

Somewhere tonight there is a street in White Plains that a real location scout will drive down one of these days, slowly, the way they do, looking at the light. That scout will have a permit application behind them and five hundred feet of notified neighbors ahead of them, and whatever house they choose will become, for a few days, the most documented building on the block. That is how the real thing announces itself. With paper. The other thing announces itself with a compliment and a request for your bank details, and it brings no trucks, no lights, no catering tent, no closing of Hamilton Avenue. Just a voice, being pleased with your house, asking for the one thing your house was never going to give it. The department has said its piece, and now so have we. If the knock comes, you will know which one it is by what it asks for first.
