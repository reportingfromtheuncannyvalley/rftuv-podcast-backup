Someone called it the world's smallest pumpkin patch.

That is either a very precise claim or no claim at all. Somebody had to measure it, or nobody did, and it stuck anyway. The way these things work is: a person writes it in a headline, and then the headline becomes the fact. Patch Hudson Valley ran it on October 10, 2026, and now it is part of the record. "The World's Smallest Pumpkin Patch." Printed. Done. LEGOLAND New York Resort, 1 Legoland Boulevard, Goshen, Orange County.

To verify it properly, you would need a working definition of pumpkin patch. That is harder than it sounds. A pumpkin patch is, at minimum, a place where pumpkins are. There is no governing body for this. The National Pumpkin Patch Association does not exist, as far as I can determine. Farmers in the Hudson Valley would probably say a patch is whatever they call a patch, and they would be largely correct, because they are the ones who planted them.

You would also need a unit of measurement. Square footage seems right. Total pumpkin count is another approach. Maybe both. You would need to survey every pumpkin patch that calls itself a patch, and there are a lot of them in October in the Hudson Valley, and most of them do not have a posted square footage, and none of them are going to welcome a guy with a measuring tape who wants to argue about definitions. So you would end up doing a lot of fieldwork for a claim that, if contested, would primarily irritate people who are already busy with October.

The practical result is that nobody verified it, and LEGOLAND has the title, and honestly, looking at the evidence, the title is probably accurate. The patch at LEGOLAND is very small. It is, by most reasonable measures, very small indeed.

The patch actually is this.

You do not go to this patch and pick a pumpkin. There are no pumpkins to pick. There is no hay, no wagon, no smell of woodsmoke, no mud on your boots. What there is, is a table, and on the table there are LEGO bricks, and you sit down and you build a pumpkin yourself, brick by brick, and when you are done, the pumpkin is yours.

Consider what that means.

A pumpkin you build out of LEGO bricks cannot rot. It will not go soft in the second week of November when the weather finally turns. It will not develop that particular gray-green mold that starts at the stem and works outward. You will not come home one day and find it has collapsed into itself overnight, the way real pumpkins do, quietly and without warning, like a small disappointment on your porch.

A pumpkin you build out of LEGO bricks cannot be eaten by deer. The deer in Orange County are bold and they are hungry in October and they do not have strong opinions about whether something is decorative or edible if it smells like pumpkin. A LEGO pumpkin does not smell like anything. The deer will leave it alone. This is a meaningful advantage.

A pumpkin you build out of LEGO bricks cannot freeze in a late October frost, which in the Hudson Valley is not a hypothetical. It can get cold fast up here. The night temperatures drop and the ground hardens and real pumpkins, if you left them outside too long, develop frost damage that turns the skin pockmarked and strange. A LEGO pumpkin will sit on your windowsill in January, if you want it to, and it will look exactly the same as it did in October. It is the most durable pumpkin you will ever own.

The size of the pumpkin depends on how you build it. But you are working with LEGO bricks, and the bricks are small, and most children are going to end up with something roughly the size of a golf ball or a tangerine. A pumpkin the size of your thumb is entirely plausible. The whole patch, all the tables and the bricks and the pumpkins in progress, could fit in a shoebox, if you wanted to try that. You could pack it up and take it home.

The smallest pumpkin patch in the world, possibly. Definitely the tidiest. There is no mud at all.

There are tryouts.

I want to be careful to state this accurately, so I will state it again: there are tryouts, at a theme park in Goshen, New York, in October of 2026, for a squad called the Zombie Cheer Crew.

The Zombie Cheer Crew is a squad of child trick-or-treaters who have been recruited, by zombies, to cheer. The recruitment event is a pep rally. At the pep rally, the LEGO Zombie Cheerleader herself is present. She is a zombie. She has pom-poms. She knows dance moves, and she will show you hers, and then she will look at yours, and she will evaluate them. Whether she issues scores or simply offers feedback is not specified in the materials I have reviewed, but the implication is clear: she is scouting.

I have covered a lot of things for this program. I have covered school board meetings where the agenda ran four hours and included a forty-five-minute discussion about font sizes on a parking sign. I have covered planning board hearings where neighbors argued about setback requirements with the intensity of people arguing about much larger things. I have never covered zombie cheerleader tryouts, but I want to treat this with the same care and fairness I would bring to any other local event, because it is a local event, and it is happening in Orange County, and the families who show up for it deserve accurate coverage.

The Zombie Cheer Crew tryouts are part of Brick-or-Treat Monster Party at LEGOLAND New York Resort. The event runs October 2 through November 2, 2026. All the activities are included with general admission. Everything happens in daylight. The Amusement Today listing for this year's event calls out the Zombie Cheer Crew by name, which means the trade press is tracking it, which means it is being taken seriously by people in the industry, which means it should be taken seriously here.

Someone is going to the pep rally this October and they are going to show a LEGO zombie their best dance moves and they are going to be evaluated. That is going to happen. And for whoever it happens to, it will be an honor. The Zombie Cheer Crew is the Zombie Cheer Crew, and not everyone gets to try out.

Now the ghost count.

Mini-Manhattan is a miniature model of New York City. It lives inside LEGOLAND, and it is made of LEGO bricks, and everything in it is small, which is to say, smaller than it already is, because everything in Mini-Manhattan is already a miniature of a real thing. The buildings are small. The streets are small. The mini-figures walking those streets are small, maybe an inch and a half tall, going about their business.

For Brick-or-Treat Monster Party, dozens of LEGO ghosts have been placed throughout Mini-Manhattan. Mini-figures dressed as trick-or-treaters are also present, working the tiny streets of the miniature city the way real trick-or-treaters work real streets, which is to say with purpose and a bag. And somewhere in all of that, there are ghosts to find.

Counting them is presented as an activity.

I respect this. I want to say that clearly. I have genuine respect for the decision to make ghost-counting an activity, because it is census work, and census work is serious, and the people who designed this event understood that children will count things if you ask them to and also if you do not ask them to. Telling a child there are ghosts to find in a miniature city is functionally equivalent to asking a census worker to enumerate a small and complicated district. They will be thorough. They will argue about what counts. They will recount.

There are real methodological questions here. How do you tell one small ghost from another? LEGO ghosts are white, semi-translucent, and small. If two are placed close together, do they count as one or two? Is there an official number, and if so, is it posted somewhere, or is the official count only revealed at the end, like the answer to a puzzle? Who counted them first? Who established the baseline? Is a recount possible, and if so, does the second count have to match the first, or is discrepancy expected and accounted for in the methodology?

These are not rhetorical questions. These are the questions you would have to answer to run a rigorous ghost census in Mini-Manhattan, and I admire the fact that LEGOLAND has set up a situation where families will work through them informally, standing over a miniature skyline, pointing at small white shapes, arguing quietly about whether that one over there counts or not. It is good family activity. It has intellectual content. And Miniland regulars, according to the materials, already know there is a spooky LEGO ghost to spot in the regular layout, which means there is a base population of ghost-spotters walking in who are already primed for this kind of work.

Good work. Serious work.

From there, field notes.

The Graveyard of Ghouls is what it sounds like, mostly: gentle ghosts and soulful skeletons arranged in a graveyard, and the tombstones have puns on them. I do not know what the puns are. I know they exist. Puns on tombstones are a tradition that predates LEGO by centuries, because people standing in graveyards apparently need something to smile about, and the people who build LEGO attractions understand this and have honored it. The Graveyard of Ghouls is a place where the puns are on the stone and the ghosts are gentle, and I think that is the right approach for a daylight Halloween event in a theme park.

Lord Vampyre is hosting a dance party. The V.I.M. Dance Party, which stands for Very Important Monster, is a party for guests who have, presumably, demonstrated monstrous dance moves. Lord Vampyre is the host, and the party is his, and the expectation is that you will bring something to the floor. I do not know what Lord Vampyre's own dance moves look like. I would imagine they are theatrical. I would imagine there is a cape involved in some of the transitions. But that is speculation, and I prefer to stick to what is confirmed: the party is real, Lord Vampyre is hosting it, and the dance moves you bring are supposed to be monstrous.

The Great Monster Chase is a 4D movie. The 4D here means the film is accompanied by physical effects: wind, bubbles, water, and others not specified in the materials I reviewed. 4D movies in theme parks are not a new technology, but they are reliably effective, because wind and water in a dark theater are surprising every time, regardless of whether you know they are coming, and children who know they are coming will still flinch and laugh when the wind hits. The Great Monster Chase runs as part of the general Brick-or-Treat programming, included with admission.

The Disco Dragon Coaster is the park's signature coaster. For Brick-or-Treat, it leaves the station with a monster soundtrack, groovy in the sense that it has a specific musical character, and party lights. There are bats overhead as it moves. The bats are presumably LEGO, or decorative, or both, and they are overhead because bats are associated with this time of year and with the particular category of fun the park is trying to create. The coaster itself is a ride. It goes fast. It comes back. That is what coasters do, and the Disco Dragon does it with a soundtrack and party lights and bats, which is a better version of what coasters do by default.

Ten candy stations are positioned around the park. Trick-or-treating starts at 2 p.m. A LEGO skeleton handles the candy distribution. I find this detail worth sitting with for a moment: someone looked at the list of characters available to hand out Halloween candy and chose the skeleton, and that is the correct choice. A skeleton handing out candy is appropriate in a way that is almost beyond argument. The skeleton has the right associations. The skeleton is available. The skeleton does not require additional costuming because the skeleton is already costumed, permanently, in its own skeleton. Good choice. Sound decision.

Wicked Sweet Way is the section of the park where the Wacky Witch's magic spell has, apparently, backfired. Every cauldron she cast over has bubbled over with sugary treats, and the result is a section of the park dedicated to the concept of too much candy, which is a concept that resonates with children and with the adults who will quietly eat some of the candy on the drive home and not mention it. The magic went wrong, the cauldrons overflowed, and now there is candy everywhere, and this is presented as a problem that everyone seems fine with.

Creatures of the Kingdom is Castle Hill, which is the park's castle area, and Lord Vampyre and Spider Lady have sent spiders and bats to take it over. The castle has been infested, and guests can build their own LEGO spider and add it to Spider Lady's web, which means the infestation is participatory. You are not just observing the hostile spider takeover of Castle Hill; you are contributing to it. You build the spider. You place it on the web. You are part of what happened to Castle Hill this October.

All the familiar rides are still running. They have creepy twists this month, and there are hidden Easter eggs placed along the routes, which is to say that while you are riding, there are things to notice that are not usually there, and whether you notice them depends on how carefully you are looking, and children who have ridden these rides before will be looking very carefully, because they know what the baseline is and they will see the differences.

Everything is in daylight. That is worth repeating. The whole event, from the zombie cheerleader tryouts to the ghost census to the skeleton handing out candy at 2 p.m., happens while the sun is up. This is the right call for an event targeting the age group LEGOLAND serves, and it also means that the spooky is always legible as play rather than as actual darkness, which is a meaningful distinction when the children involved are small enough to have strong opinions about the distinction. Daylight Halloween is a design philosophy, and it is a sound one.

The event is actually asking this question, underneath all of it.

What is it that gathers people in a field in October?

The Hudson Valley has real pumpkin patches. It has the kind of patches that get measured in acres, where the mud on your boots is real mud and the pumpkins weigh what pumpkins weigh and the hay wagon moves slowly over uneven ground and the farm stand sells cider in paper cups. People go to those patches because the patch is a ritual. It is October, and you are supposed to be somewhere that looks like October, and a field with pumpkins looks like October, and so you go.

LEGOLAND has a patch where the pumpkins are built from bricks and fit in your hand, and dozens of plastic ghosts are hidden in a miniature city, and a zombie is waiting to evaluate your dancing. And people will go to that, too. They will drive to Goshen, Orange County, 1 Legoland Boulevard, and they will count the ghosts and they will build the pumpkins and they will show the zombie their moves. They will do all of that.

I think the answer to the question is simpler than it might seem. People will gather anywhere in October in the Hudson Valley. That is just what happens in October up here. The valley turns and the light goes sideways and there is something in the air that makes people want to be somewhere specific with the people they know. A real field works. A miniature field works. A table with LEGO bricks works. The gathering is the point.

And who counted the ghosts first?

Someone did. Some family came through early in October, maybe right after the event opened on the 2nd, before the crowds got going, and they walked through Mini-Manhattan slowly, and one of the kids started counting, and someone else started keeping track on their hand or in their head, and they got to a number and they said that number out loud, and nobody could confirm it was right, and they argued about it for a little while, and then they moved on to the candy stations.

That first count is out there. It is part of what October in the Hudson Valley becomes, year by year: an accumulation of small rituals, some of them in fields with real pumpkins and real mud, and some of them in a clean bright park in Goshen where the smallest pumpkins in the world do not rot and the ghosts are plastic and the counting is the whole point.
