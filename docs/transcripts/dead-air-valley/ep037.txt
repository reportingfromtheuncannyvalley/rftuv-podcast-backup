Silas: The driver did the thing the training says to do. It is the whole story.

Silas: Consider what a garbage truck actually is, mechanically, because the decision only makes sense once you do. It is a heavy steel box on wheels, and inside that box a hydraulic compactor spends the day squeezing everything smaller. The machinery that does the squeezing runs on hydraulic lines full of pressurized fluid. Under the cab sits a fuel tank. A fire loose inside that box is not a campfire. It is a fire shopping for something bigger to become. Given time, it finds the lines. Given more time, it finds the tank. The reporting made the point in one clean piece of logic, and I cannot improve on it. Dumping the load onto the roadway keeps the flames inside the compactor from ever reaching the fuel or the hydraulics. You give up the trash. You keep the truck. You keep the street. You keep everybody standing on it.

Silas: These trucks are not disposable equipment, either. A modern collection truck is a specialized machine, built for exactly one job, expensive to buy and slow to replace, and a hauler or a town that loses one does not simply order another by Friday. So the arithmetic of that Thursday morning was not close. On one side, a load of garbage, already thrown away once by the people who owned it. On the other side, the truck, the driver, the road, and every house the fire might have introduced itself to if it had been given the run of a fuel tank. The driver did the arithmetic instantly, the way you do when the arithmetic has been written into your training. The load went on the pavement.

Silas: The Monsey Fire Department came. Silas: There is a tendency, in stories like this one, to treat the driver as a footnote and the fire as the character. I would like to resist that.
