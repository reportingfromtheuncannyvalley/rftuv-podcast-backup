Silas Van Wyck: The town has two things working simultaneously and in apparent harmony. The second is the Metro-North Hudson Line, which stops at Tarrytown, one town over, and runs regular service into Grand Central.

Silas Van Wyck: Both facts are true. Neither cancels the other.

The Headless Horseman is part of the economy, and in October, the economy is open for business.

Silas Van Wyck: October in Sleepy Hollow has a particular quality that is difficult to describe without sounding like a tourism pamphlet, and yet the tourism pamphlets have a point. The leaves in the Hudson Valley turn in earnest by the second week of the month. The light comes off the river at a low angle in the afternoons, the kind of light that makes everything look like it is being remembered rather than observed. The trees along Palmer Avenue go gold and orange and occasionally a deep red that local residents describe as routine and that everyone else photographs. The air smells like leaves and cold water and, on festival weekends, food trucks. The town does not pause for any of this. The town has a schedule.

Silas Van Wyck: This year, the business in question includes a S U P Witches Festival.

Silas Van Wyck: S U P stands for stand-up paddleboard. A paddleboard is a long, flat board, wider than a surfboard and more stable, designed for standing upright on calm or semi-calm water. You propel it with a long paddle. It is a sport that requires some experience, a certain comfort with balance, and, in October on the Hudson River, a tolerance for cold.

Silas Van Wyck: The seventh annual S U P Witches Festival takes place at Kingsland Point Park on Saturday, October tenth, twenty twenty-six, from noon to five in the afternoon. Rain date is Sunday, October eleventh. Kingsland Point Park sits on the Hudson River at two ninety-nine Palmer Avenue, in the part of Sleepy Hollow where the river opens up and the light in the afternoon goes sideways in that particular Hudson Valley way that landscape painters spent the entire nineteenth century trying to describe.

Silas Van Wyck: At two o'clock in the afternoon, the main event begins. Hundreds of witches climb onto paddleboards and kayaks and paddle the Hudson River. They are in full costume. There is a check-in table. From the shore, the effect is what you might expect if you expected this sort of thing, which the town now does. The water fills with color: black robes, pointed hats, capes in deep green and purple and red, all moving in loose formation across the surface of the river while the Cuomo Bridge sits in the background and the lighthouse watches from the point. Spectators line the shore. Some of them are also in costume. Nobody explains this to anyone. It is simply the two-o'clock hour on the second Saturday of October, in Sleepy Hollow, on the Hudson.

Silas Van Wyck: Kingsland Point Park is a forty-two-acre wedge of land where the river bends and you can see across to the Tappan Zee, the wide part of the Hudson where the old bridge stood and where the Governor Mario M. Cuomo Bridge now carries the Thruway north. The park has a lighthouse, built in eighteen eighty-three, decommissioned but still standing, and correctly placed, by any reasonable standard, for a day involving witches. On festival day, the lawn between the tree line and the water fills with vendors and spectators and the organized activity of a town that has done this before. People set up chairs. People eat. The bands play. Somewhere near the shore, witches in full costume are checking in, adjusting their hats, and looking at the river.

Silas Van Wyck: The S U P Witches Festival is one of three consecutive Saturdays of outdoor festivals that Kingsland Point Park hosts each fall. The sequence begins with Sleepy Hollow Oktoberfest, followed by the Sleepy Hollow Music Festival, and concludes with the S U P Witches Festival. The town has organized its calendar around the proposition that October is not a month but a production.

Silas Van Wyck: Sleepy Hollow Oktoberfest runs the first Saturday of October. It occupies the same park, the same lawn, the same view of the Tappan Zee. There are vendors, there is beer, there are presumably people in lederhosen, and there is the particular quality of an outdoor festival in early October in the Hudson Valley, which is to say the weather is fine and the light is good and everyone is aware, at some level, that they are standing on the edge of a haunted glen. The Oktoberfest does not make much of this. It is an Oktoberfest. It moves along.

Silas Van Wyck: The Sleepy Hollow Music Festival follows the next Saturday. It is, by design, a music festival: bands, lawn space, the same Hudson River backdrop. The town uses it as the bridge between the beer and the witches. This is sound programming logic. By the time the third Saturday arrives and hundreds of costumed paddlers are staging at the water's edge, the park has had two weekends of practice. The crews know where to put the tables. The vendors know the site. The river, for its part, is unchanged.

Silas Van Wyck: This year, the festival runs from noon to five. The paddling window, the two-o'clock hour, is when the witches are on the water. Before and after that, there is a festival.

Silas Van Wyck: Live music begins at one. Might and the Weevils play from one to two-fifteen. The Incumbents play from three to four-thirty. There are vendors. There are food trucks. There is a live telling of the Legend of Sleepy Hollow, which presumably includes the Horseman, the pumpkin, and the bridge, performed in a park that sits within what Irving described as a sleepy, sequestered glen.

Paddling witches (that is, people who are participating in the on-water portion of the event) do not need a separate festival ticket. The ticket to be on the water gets you into the festival.

Silas Van Wyck: The S U P Witches Festival does not exist in isolation. It is one point in a regional October economy that runs from the Catskills to the Sound.

Silas Van Wyck: Nyack, twelve miles south on the Hudson, holds its thirty-seventh annual Halloween parade on October twenty-fourth. Nyack's parade has been running since the late nineteen eighties, and it has the character of an event that predates its own Instagram documentation by enough years that the costumes tend toward the handmade and the elaborate rather than the purchased. It is a walking parade through the village's main streets, open to anyone in costume, and it draws a crowd that arrives early and stays late. The downtown blocks are lined. If you are going, go before six.

Silas Van Wyck: Mamaroneck, on the Sound side of Westchester, holds its Spooktacular on October twenty-fifth. The Mamaroneck Spooktacular is organized around the premise that Halloween is primarily for children who are old enough to have opinions about their costumes but young enough to still need a parent nearby. There are activities, a parade of its own, and the concentrated enthusiasm of a community event where the volunteers outnumber the attendees and everyone is fine with this.

Silas Van Wyck: Kingston, the old Dutch city ninety miles up the Hudson, holds the Burning of Kingston from October fifteenth through eighteenth. In seventeen seventy-seven, British forces burned most of the city. Kingston has been re-enacting this, with some variation in solemnity, ever since. The event includes historical reenactors, walking tours, and period demonstrations through the Stockade District, which is the oldest part of the city and which survived the original burning better than the rest. It is not, technically, a Halloween event. It is in October, and it involves fire. The distinction may be academic.

Silas Van Wyck: Sleepy Hollow Cemetery, where Washington Irving is buried, offers guided tours. The cemetery is large, historic, and the resting place of, among others, Andrew Carnegie, William Rockefeller, and the author of the story that gave this particular town its particular autumn economy. Irving's grave is marked. The tours run through October. Philipsburg Manor, a restored seventeenth-century Dutch manor house about a mile north of the cemetery, runs Halloween programming through the season: lantern-lit tours, period interpretations, the kind of October experience that regional tourism publications describe as "atmospheric" and that is, by any reasonable standard, actually atmospheric.

Silas Van Wyck: Hudson Valley residents tracking the full range of spooky season options are also watching Salem, Massachusetts, though from a distance. Salem is a four-hour drive from Sleepy Hollow. The valley's witches are making their own October. Salem's witches are making theirs.
