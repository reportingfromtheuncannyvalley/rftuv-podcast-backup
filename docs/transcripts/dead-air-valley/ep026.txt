Dead Air Valley: "The Cloud That Stayed"

On Sunday night, October 25, 2026, a fireball crossed over Oneonta, New York. A camera caught it. Shortly after it was gone, a cloud was hanging in the path where it had been.

That is the fact this episode follows. The light left. The cloud did not.

The Ordinary Sunday Night

Late October in Oneonta is ordinary in the way that late October in any small city in the Susquehanna Valley is ordinary. The nights come early now. Otsego County sits far enough upstate that when you step outside on a clear night, there is a lot of sky.

Cameras were rolling across Upstate New York over the weekend of October 23, 2026. On Sunday night, October 25, a fireball crossed over Oneonta on video. Another picture was taken, of the sky after, and what that picture showed is what this episode is about.

The night before a full moon, which is relevant and we will get to it. A Sunday in late October, inside what NASA describes as the autumn fireball period, which runs from September through November. People home. The sky doing something that was, by the technical definitions we use, extraordinary, even if no one on the ground knew it yet.

That is the surface. Now the streak.

The Streak

Upstate meteorologist Bill Kardas shared two videos of a fireball flying across the sky in Oneonta. One of those videos came from one of his social media followers in Oneonta. Kardas passed both along, the way meteorologists who watch conditions over a region do when something worth watching shows up in a viewer's camera roll.

Let's establish what we are talking about when we say fireball, because the word is not casual here. It has a definition. Per NASA, as carried by the 94.3 Lite FM report on this event: a fireball is an unusually bright meteor that reaches a visual magnitude of minus three or brighter when seen at the observer's zenith. That threshold is the line that separates a fireball from an ordinary meteor, and it is a meaningful line.

Objects causing fireball events can exceed one meter in size. Fireballs that explode in the atmosphere are technically referred to as bolides, though the terms are often used interchangeably. The bolide distinction matters in the technical literature because an explosion in the atmosphere produces a different signature than a fireball that simply burns through, but from the ground the word people reach for is fireball, and for this episode that word does the necessary work. The general background from NASA, again as carried by the same account: objects causing fireballs are usually not large enough to survive passage through the atmosphere intact, although fragments, called meteorites, are sometimes recovered on the ground. None of those specifics, whether it exceeded a meter, whether it exploded, whether anything landed, appear in any source for the Oneonta event. That background is context for what kind of object we are dealing with in general. For what happened over Oneonta specifically, the sources give us what the videos gave Kardas: the streak, on video, twice.

Fireballs, definitionally, burn bright and brief. They are the meteor that stands out from the background, and then they are over. The sky returns to whatever it was before. That is what makes what came next worth talking about.

The Second Town

Before we get to the cloud, there is Carlton.

Carlton, New York, over two hundred miles from Oneonta, also had a fireball on camera that same Sunday night. Kardas shared that video too, this one from a follower named Rick. Two videos from Oneonta, one video from Carlton, all of them on a Sunday night in late October, all of them moving through Kardas's social media feed and then through 94.3 Lite FM's account of the weekend.

This episode has to be straight with you, because the sources are, and we follow the sources.

The two sightings, Oneonta and Carlton, are two sightings on the same night. That is what the sources establish. The sources do not establish whether they were the same widely visible event, one fireball seen from different angles two hundred miles apart, or two separate fireballs on the same night. That question is open. The American Meteor Society event reporting that might have settled it was not the page available in the sources read for this episode. What was available was the weekly outlook for October 24 through 30, 2026. That document is a general activity and conditions forecast for the full week. It tells you whether the week is favorable or unfavorable for meteor observing, which streams are active, and what the moon is doing. It does not record or analyze specific events on specific nights. So the AMS page in the sources is useful for understanding the week's sky context and nothing more. It is not an event report for October 25, and it does not say anything about the Oneonta fireball or the Carlton fireball individually.

So the sources present two sightings, over two hundred miles apart, on the same night. And they leave it there. This script leaves it there too. It is not a gap to fill in with inference. It is an honest open question in a story that already has plenty that is strange enough on its own terms.

Carlton and Oneonta. Two videos, two towns, the same night. Same kind of sky event, by the look of it. Whether the same rock or not: the sources do not know, and neither do we.

The Cloud That Stayed

Now the detail that carries this episode.

Shortly after the Oneonta fireball video, a picture was posted of unique clouds in the night sky. A social media follower posted it. Kardas explained what he thought he was looking at.

Here is his quote, from the 94.3 Lite FM account of this event, used once, as he said it:

"Spotted shortly after the fireball in its path. This cloud is likely debris from the space rock burning in earth's atmosphere."

That is what Kardas said. Not confirmed debris. Not measured debris. Likely debris. That word is doing real work in that sentence and it should stay in yours when you think about what you just read.

A photograph of a cloud hanging in the path of a fireball, shortly after the fireball has gone, is a strange thing to be looking at. Strange partly because of what it suggests and partly because of the plainness of the suggestion. Kardas is not hedging in a bureaucratic way. He is telling you what the cloud most probably is: likely debris from the space rock burning in the atmosphere. His word, used exactly as he used it.

The ordinary explanation for the cloud, if you accept the "likely" and do not upgrade it to more than that, is that you are looking at the aftermath of a disintegration. A space rock enters the atmosphere. The atmosphere wins. The rock does not survive intact. What is left is not always nothing. Sometimes what is left is something you can photograph.

What makes this particular photograph worth the name of an episode is the combination of things it requires you to hold at once. A fireball is defined by brightness and brevity. It is the meteor that goes bright enough to stop you, and then it is over. The sky goes back to being the sky. That is the deal. What the photograph after the Oneonta fireball shows is the sky not quite going back to being the sky. There is something in the path. Likely debris. In a cloud. In the frame. And someone thought to take the picture.

A fireball is supposed to be the thing that disappears. This one left something in the air.

That is not a ghost story. Kardas's explanation is the straightforward atmospheric one, and straightforward atmospheric explanations are the ones we use here. But the explanation does not make the photograph less odd to sit with. The cloud is in the path. The fireball is gone. The cloud is there.

The Season and the Moon

There is context for why this particular week, on this particular Sunday night, a fireball bright enough to film was crossing over Otsego County.

Start with the calendar. NASA, as cited in the 94.3 Lite FM report, identifies two fireball periods in the year. The peak runs from February through April. The second window runs from September through November, and it overlaps with major meteor streams, among them the Southern Taurids and the Northern Taurids. The Southern Taurids reach their maximum on November 5. The Northern Taurids reach theirs on November 12. According to the American Meteor Society's outlook for the week of October 24 through 30, 2026, this stretch leading up to those maxima is a good time to try to view slow-moving meteors such as the Taurids.

No source assigns the Oneonta fireball to the Taurids specifically. That assignment does not come from anything in the sources for this episode, so we are not making it. What the season establishes is simpler: late October is, in general, a time when the Earth's path through space runs through material that produces fireball activity. Sunday, October 25 was inside that window.

Now the moon, which is part of the week's context.

The American Meteor Society's outlook for that week notes that the moon reached its full phase on Monday, October 26. At that point, the moon lay above the horizon all night, and bright moonlight would obscure all but the brighter meteors. The outlook describes it as a difficult week for meteor observing. It is worth being clear about what that characterization is and is not: it is a general forward-looking assessment of sky conditions for the week of October 24 through 30. The Society publishes these outlooks as planning guidance, covering activity levels and viewing conditions across the full week. The outlook does not go back and assess what happened on any particular night within the week. What it offers here is background context, not event analysis.

The Oneonta fireball was on Sunday night, October 25, the night before the full moon. The moon was already very bright, already washing out the sky for fainter objects. The AMS outlook's point holds for that night as well as the nights that followed: during a stretch of near-full and full moonlight, the meteors that get seen are the ones bright enough to clear the moonlight. The Oneonta sighting happened in that context.

That is the week's sky condition as the sources describe it. A fireball on a night like that is not invisible. A fainter meteor would be.

Five Nights Before Halloween

The light is gone now. The cloud is still there in the photograph, but the fireball itself was brief, as all fireballs are by definition. The distance between Oneonta and Carlton is still over two hundred miles, and the question of whether one event produced both sightings or two events did is still open. The sources did not settle it. This episode does not settle it.

94.3 Lite FM asked readers who saw a fireball on Sunday night, October 25, to share where in New York they were and around what time, inviting them to respond through the station's mobile app. That is how a call like this works: the station names the event and opens the channel, and listeners respond with a location and a time if they have one. Each response places a point on the map of where the fireball was seen. A sighting from one town does not tell you how far the event extended. A cluster of responses from towns spread across several counties starts to tell you. The station asked. The question of whether responses came in, and from which towns, is not answered in the sources for this episode. We have Oneonta. We have Carlton. We have the cloud.

Oneonta is in Otsego County, in the Susquehanna Valley. The show rotates through the valley and its neighbors, and this week that rotation moved west: from Saugerties on October 25, to Poughquag on October 26, to Carmel in Putnam County on October 27, to Beacon in Dutchess County on October 28, and now to Otsego County on October 29. Each of those places is under the same sky. On Sunday night, October 25, Otsego County had the better view.

Halloween is Saturday, October 31. Two days from now. The fireball night was five nights before Halloween, which puts it squarely inside the autumn fireball window NASA describes, and squarely inside the kind of week when the sky in Upstate New York tends to be doing things that are worth looking at if you happen to have a camera pointed up.

The strange thing about this story is not that a fireball crossed the sky. Fireballs are uncommon but they happen; they happen more often in some seasons than others; they happen and are missed more often than they are caught on camera, especially in a week when the moon is nearly full and washing out everything that is not bright enough to fight through. The strange thing is the cloud. The photograph of the cloud, in the path, shortly after.

Kardas saw it, explained it, and posted the explanation alongside the videos. A meteorologist with a regional following who shares what his followers send him, doing exactly that. His followers sent him the streak. They sent him the cloud after. He explained both and shared both. The sourcing chain for this story is not long: two videos, a picture, one meteorologist, one station, and the plain fact of what the photograph shows.

What the photograph shows is the sky in Oneonta keeping the record of something that was already gone. A fireball is defined, in the moment, by the light it produces. The light is what makes it a fireball rather than a rock in the dark. The light is also, almost always, all there is. The rock does not survive. The light does not last. The sky goes back to whatever it was. That is the deal, every time.

Except this time there was a cloud in the path. Likely debris from the space rock burning in earth's atmosphere. That is the explanation. The explanation is ordinary enough, as things go. The photograph is still there.
