Silas Van Wyck: NARRATOR: SILAS VAN WYCK

Silas Van Wyck: There is a place in Ballston Spa, New York, that has spent thirty-six years in the business of frightening people. That is the entire business model. You drive out to Route sixty seven in Saratoga County, you park the car, and you pay someone to scare you. The people who work there are trained to unsettle you. The environment is engineered to disorient you. The whole operation, running since nineteen ninety one, exists specifically to make you feel like something has gone wrong.

Silas Van Wyck: And then, on a Saturday night this October, that same place had to stop and address a guest who had actually made something go wrong.

Silas Van Wyck: Not a ghost, not a malfunction, a guest.

Silas Van Wyck: The place is called Double M Haunted Hayrides and Haunted Houses. The situation is, depending on your perspective, a minor property dispute with a very public resolution, or one of the more precisely targeted public callouts in the recent history of the Capital Region's fall entertainment calendar. Either way, it involves a list of curses so small they might be the most unsettling thing Double M has produced this season.

Silas Van Wyck: This is Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. I'm Silas Van Wyck.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE PLACE AND ITS HISTORY]

Silas Van Wyck: Let's start with where we are, because the geography matters.

Silas Van Wyck: Ballston Spa sits in Saratoga County. That is the Capital Region, not the Hudson Valley proper, though Hudson Valley audiences make the drive north. You may know the names that anchor the haunted attraction circuit closer to home: Kevin McCurdy's Haunted Mansion in Wappingers Falls, the Headless Horseman in Ulster Park. Those are the regional reference points. Double M is a step further north, and for people who follow this particular corner of fall tourism, the trip is considered worth it.

Silas Van Wyck: Double M Haunted Hayrides and Haunted Houses has been operating at its Route sixty seven location in Ballston Spa since nineteen ninety one. This year is the thirty-sixth season. That number is worth sitting with for a moment, because the haunted attraction industry is not known for quiet longevity. These operations run on seasonal staff, compressed revenue windows, weather, and the annual gamble that enough people want to be frightened in October to make the math work. Thirty-six consecutive seasons in one location, in one region, is a real record.

Silas Van Wyck: The format at Double M is layered. The main event is a tractor-drawn hayride, roughly thirty to forty minutes, moving through designed environments in the dark. After the hayride, there are walk-through haunted houses and a midway. Reservations are required. It is not a drop-in attraction. The guests who show up on any given night have planned to be there. They have booked a time. They have driven to Saratoga County on purpose.

Silas Van Wyck: This is not a spontaneous haunt. It is a curated experience with a thirty-six-year track record, a regional reputation, and a guest list that shows up expecting to be scared by professionals. Most of them, by Double M's own account, do exactly what guests are supposed to do. They scream, they laugh, they grab the arm of whoever they came with, and they leave talking about the parts that got them.

Silas Van Wyck: One guest, on one Saturday night this October, allegedly did something else entirely.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE SATURDAY NIGHT ALLEGATION]

Silas Van Wyck: What follows is drawn from a post made by Double M Haunted Hayrides and Haunted Houses, as reported by Hudson Valley Country on October thirteenth, twenty twenty six, and corroborated by two regional radio stations, one hundred four point five The Team and Q105.seven, which summarized the same post. The attraction itself is the source. The incident is alleged. No guest name is known. No outcome beyond the post has been reported.

Silas Van Wyck: Per the attraction's own account: on a Saturday night, a guest pushed past one of Double M's actresses. In the course of doing so, that guest allegedly wrestled a prop away from her. And then that guest, along with the prop, left.

Silas Van Wyck: That is the allegation as reported. No prop is described by name. No confrontation beyond that moment has been reported. No involvement by any outside authority has been described. What is known is that a woman who came to work at a haunted attraction that night, to perform her role in a thirty-six-year-old entertainment operation, allegedly had someone take something from her, and that person left.

Silas Van Wyck: What happened next was the post.

Silas Van Wyck: Double M did not describe the prop. They did not name the guest. They did not announce consequences. They issued a reminder, and then they issued a list.

Silas Van Wyck: The reminder, paraphrased from their post, made a point that feels obvious but apparently needed saying. The actors at Double M are there to entertain. They are there to scare. They are not there to be harassed. They are not there to be disrespected. They are not there to have equipment taken from them. The post acknowledged, as these things tend to do, that the vast majority of guests are wonderful. The problem, as the post framed it, is the occasional person who walks into any situation as if the rules are decorative. As if the social contract is for other people. As if a haunted attraction, because it is dark and strange and designed to feel out of control, is a place where ordinary standards of behavior no longer apply.

Silas Van Wyck: They are not. The rules apply. The contract holds. And then came the list.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE REMINDER UNDER THE JOKE]

Silas Van Wyck: Before we get to the curses themselves, it is worth staying with the straight part of the post for a moment, because the joke only works if the straight part is real.

Silas Van Wyck: There is a particular challenge built into the haunted attraction format. The entire design of the experience is predicated on removing certain normal cues. It is dark. Strangers are in close proximity to each other and to actors. The scenario is explicitly one where something feels wrong. That is the product. The customer is purchasing a controlled version of unease.

Silas Van Wyck: The industry norm, consistent across operations like Double M, is a clear boundary: guests do not touch actors, and actors are trained not to touch guests. That line holds the whole thing together. It is what makes the experience safe for everyone involved. The actors are doing a job. A physically and emotionally demanding job, performed in costume, in the dark, in close contact with strangers, on a compressed seasonal schedule. They are professionals doing what they were hired to do.

Silas Van Wyck: When someone crosses that line, the experience that everyone else paid for and showed up to enjoy gets interrupted. The actress who allegedly had a prop taken from her that Saturday night was not a prop herself. She was a person at work.

Silas Van Wyck: Double M's post made that point. It made it clearly. And then, having made it, they made their list.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE CURSE LIST AND WHY EACH WISH IS SO SMALL]

Silas Van Wyck: The list is the thing people are talking about. And the reason people are talking about it is the scale.

Silas Van Wyck: Double M did not wish this person harm. They did not wish professional ruin, or social embarrassment, or any of the escalating consequences you might expect from an organization that had just had a piece of its equipment allegedly stolen. What they wished was smaller than that. Much smaller. Deliberately, specifically, surgically small.

Silas Van Wyck: A pillow that is warm on both sides. That is one of the curses.

Silas Van Wyck: Think about that for a moment. The wish is not for something dramatic. It is for something that will happen in the dark, when the person is alone, when they reach for the cool side of the pillow and find that it does not exist. The cool side is always there. It is one of the minor physical reliefs that people count on without knowing they count on it, until the night they flip the pillow and find the same temperature waiting on the other side. Nothing catastrophic. Just the absence of a small comfort, recurring, indefinitely.

Silas Van Wyck: The list continues in the same register. Stubbing a toe every morning. Not a broken bone, not a fall, just the particular sharp pain of catching a toe on a piece of furniture first thing in the day, the pain that is over in seconds but colors the following minutes, repeated every morning for the foreseeable future.

Silas Van Wyck: Flat soda. You open the bottle and the fizz is gone. Not spoiled, not wrong, just flat. A beverage that promised one thing and delivered slightly less.

Silas Van Wyck: Slightly damp socks. Not wet, not soaked, damp. The way socks feel when you put them on and cannot tell if they are actually damp or if you are imagining it, and you spend the first twenty minutes of your morning uncertain about your own feet.

Silas Van Wyck: Toast landing butter side down. Every time. The particular arithmetic of that one, the way it seems to defy probability, the way it happens at the exact moment you were not paying attention, and now you have to decide whether to eat it anyway or start over, and either choice is a small defeat.

Silas Van Wyck: Pizza cheese sticking to the box. Not a ruined pizza. A pizza that is ninety-five percent correct, except for the portion that is now adhered to the cardboard lid, the portion you ordered, the portion you were looking forward to, the portion that requires a fork or a finger to retrieve in some diminished form from the inside of a box.

Silas Van Wyck: A phone charger that only works in one position.

Silas Van Wyck: That one does not need elaboration. Everyone who has owned a phone charger for long enough knows exactly what this means. The cable that has to be held at a specific angle, plugged in with a specific pressure, and left in a specific orientation, and if you move it, if you adjust it even slightly, you wake up to a phone at forty percent and a quiet fury that has nowhere to go.

Silas Van Wyck: The curses are not catastrophes. They are the texture of a life going slightly wrong at regular intervals. A life in which small things consistently fail to deliver. In which the minor comforts are unreliable, in which the low-stakes moments carry a faint persistent friction.

Silas Van Wyck: The genius of this list, if you want to call it that, is that it requires no extraordinary circumstances to fulfill. These are things that happen to people anyway. Double M is essentially wishing that this particular person experiences the normal background rate of daily mild disappointment at a slightly elevated frequency, and never gets to chalk it up to coincidence.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE FINAL WISH]

Silas Van Wyck: And then comes the last item on the list. And this one is different.

Silas Van Wyck: The final wish, paraphrased from the post: that every haunted house this person visits from now on will be staffed by actors who know exactly who they are.

Silas Van Wyck: That is the one that only a haunted attraction could write.

Silas Van Wyck: Everything else on the list is a universal small misery. Warm pillows and damp socks happen to people who have never set foot in a haunted hayride. But this last wish belongs specifically to the world Double M inhabits. It assumes the person will keep going to haunted attractions. It does not wish them away from the experience. It wishes them into it, repeatedly, with the specific additional layer that the people whose job it is to frighten them will have context.

Silas Van Wyck: The actors at haunted houses work in environments designed to give them a kind of anonymous authority. You do not know who they are under the makeup and the costume and the dark. The inversion that the final wish proposes is that this person, specifically, will walk into those environments and the actors will know them. The power dynamic that makes haunted attractions work, the designed disorientation, the anonymity of the performer, shifts.

Silas Van Wyck: Whether that wish is logistically achievable is not the point. The point is that it is the one curse only someone inside the industry could construct. It uses the specific mechanics of the art form as the instrument of the wish. That is, by any measure, a well-crafted ending to a list.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE WIDER OCTOBER PROBLEM]

Silas Van Wyck: What happened at Double M on that Saturday night, as alleged, is not unique to Ballston Spa. It is one instance of a recurring seasonal challenge.

Silas Van Wyck: October is peak season for haunted attractions across the region. Operations like Double M run a compressed schedule. The revenue window is short. The staff is largely seasonal. The environment is designed to push guests into a state of controlled excitement, darkness, and proximity to strangers.

Silas Van Wyck: Close contact is, by design, part of the experience. The corridors of a walk-through haunted house are narrow. Actors are nearby. The sounds and the lighting are calibrated to keep guests off-balance. The industry norm - that guests do not touch actors and actors are trained not to touch guests - exists because that proximity is real and continuous across every operating night.

Silas Van Wyck: The people working these events are doing so under conditions that are physically demanding and atmospherically strange. They are in costume. They are in character. They are standing in dark corners and jumping out and using their voices and their bodies to perform, repeatedly, for hours, for groups of strangers who arrive pre-excited and leave through a midway.

Silas Van Wyck: Most guests, as Double M acknowledged and as any operator in this industry would tell you, are fine. They came for the experience. They engage with the experience. They respect the people providing it. The problem is the subset of guests who arrive in any public-facing environment with the apparent belief that paid admission is a general permission slip. That the cost of a ticket extends to the staff and the props and the whole constructed reality of the place.

Silas Van Wyck: The actresses working hayride nights in October on Route sixty seven in Saratoga County are not decorative elements. They are employees doing a seasonal job in a regional entertainment tradition that is thirty-six years old. Taking something from one of them is not part of the experience. It is just taking something.

Silas Van Wyck: [THE CLOSE]

Silas Van Wyck: As of the reporting on October thirteenth, twenty twenty six, the prop has not been returned. The list remains public.

Silas Van Wyck: Double M Haunted Hayrides and Haunted Houses will continue operating through the rest of the month. Guests will arrive with reservations, will ride the tractor-drawn hayride through the dark, will walk through the haunted houses, will have the experience the operation has been providing since nineteen ninety one.

Silas Van Wyck: Some of those guests will have seen the curse list before they arrive. They will have read about the warm pillow, the damp socks, the charger that only works sideways. They will have read the final wish about the actors who know exactly who you are. And they will arrive at the ticket window having already been reminded, by a piece of writing funnier and more specific than any sign at a gate, that the people working this attraction are people, and that the rules apply to everyone, and that Double M, if pressed, has a gift for proportionate consequences.

Silas Van Wyck: That is worth something, in terms of crowd management. People remember stories. They remember jokes. They remember a list that wished someone a pillow warm on both sides more clearly than they remember a laminated notice about acceptable behavior.

Silas Van Wyck: The prop is still gone. But the list is still there. And every future guest who reads it before buying a ticket has already been told, in the clearest possible terms, what kind of place this is, what kind of people work there, and how specifically and creatively the attraction will respond if you mistake the dark for an excuse.

Silas Van Wyck: The curses are small on purpose. The point was never the size of the punishment. The point was the precision of the aim.
