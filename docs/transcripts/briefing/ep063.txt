The polls closed a day ago. Tonight, part of the picture is settled and part of it is still being counted, in one county literally a bag at a time. We will finish with the results board, local and national. First, the advertising, because the latest update of the count of what this election's ads were made of has now been published.

The Wesleyan Media Project, which tracks political advertising, published the latest update of its count on October twenty ninth: more than one hundred eighty-seven million dollars spent across at least two hundred eighty-nine ads that were generated or enhanced by AI. The genre has a name now, slopaganda, a term AI researchers coined last year for political ads built with generative artificial intelligence: realistic clips and images of things that never happened. In twenty twenty-four, the same count came to under fifty thousand dollars. More than ninety percent of this year's spending came from Republican candidates, a party committee, or Republican-leaning groups. Of the ads the project counted through mid October, about twenty-seven percent carried any disclosure that AI was involved; most carried none.

The examples are documented, not hypothetical. In Texas, the Senate campaign of Ken Paxton circulated an AI video of his opponent, James Talarico, saying things he never said. Here in New York, a fake video of Governor Kathy Hochul was posted by her Republican challenger, Bruce Blakeman. Both were catalogued in reporting on the project's figures this fall.

The law that might police them is a patchwork: there is no comprehensive federal rule on AI in political ads, while around thirty states have written their own, all different. So this cycle's synthetic ads were policed mainly by the platforms themselves, each drawing its line in a different place.

At midday, Apple was warning its own investors, in a formal filing, that AI agents could behave in unintended and unpredictable ways. Tonight, the market's answer to that warning is on sale.

CrowdStrike used its conference in Barcelona yesterday to introduce what it calls an AI kill switch, built into its Falcon Guardian product. The company says an authorized administrator can stop an agent that has gone off course, block it from starting again, and cut off its network access, with each action written into an audit log. For now, the stop happens at the gateway its customers' agents pass through; stopping an agent on the machine itself comes later, the company says.

CrowdStrike also agreed to buy two smaller firms. Fiddler AI watches what agents do and explains their decisions afterward, a flight recorder for every agent, in the words of CrowdStrike's chief executive, George Kurtz. Dreadnode attacks AI systems on purpose, testing them for weaknesses before they ship. CrowdStrike did not disclose the prices. The Wall Street Journal reports about four hundred million dollars for Fiddler and about ninety-five million for Dreadnode, four hundred ninety-five million together, mostly in cash. Neither deal has closed; the company expects both to finish by the end of January.

Now to Westchester County, where yesterday's count is still not finished.

More than fifty-seven thousand Election Day ballots could not be scanned at the polls yesterday, after the county's scanners rejected them. Voters completed paper ballots, and those ballots went into sealed emergency bags. At eleven this morning, under an order from State Supreme Court Justice Paul Marx, workers in Ardsley began scanning them, in a count the order says runs uninterrupted until it is complete. As of late this afternoon, local reporting from the county said a significant number of those ballots were still uncounted, and several races remain incomplete.

The race being watched is the Seventeenth Congressional District. Democrat Cait Conley leads Republican Mike Lawler, and Lawler conceded on election night, telling supporters the uncounted Westchester ballots would only widen her lead. The Associated Press, though, has still not called the race, and nothing is official until the count finishes and the county certifies it. In the Sixteenth District, the AP called the race for George Latimer even with the count incomplete, and at least one county legislature race is still waiting on the same bags.

On what went wrong, the state Board of Elections says a late change in one local contest, the village justice race in Ardsley, changed the identifying codes on ballots across the county, and the new programming did not reach every scanner. Machines still running the original programming could not recognize the ballots. County Executive Ken Jenkins has promised a bipartisan investigation, and a county election commissioner says investigators will decide whether the fault sits with the county board or with the ballot printer. What officials have said from the start, and repeated today, is the simpler part: no voter was turned away, the ballots were kept secure, and every lawful ballot will be counted.

The rest of the board, quickly. The Associated Press called the Senate for the Democrats late last night and the House early this morning, so Democrats will hold both houses of Congress when the new Congress sits in January, across from a Republican president. The AP has also called Governor Kathy Hochul's reelection here in New York. And in State Senate District Thirty-nine, which takes in Beacon, Poughkeepsie, and Newburgh, Democrat Lisa Kaul defeated incumbent Rob Rolison on unofficial totals, by nearly twelve thousand votes, and Rolison has congratulated her. Every one of these results stays unofficial until it is certified.
