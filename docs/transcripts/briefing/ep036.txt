We start with the attorney general's race, covered today in a piece by Callahan Blackwood at rftuv.ai, verified and read in full for this edition.

The New York attorney general is the state's chief legal officer, running the Medicaid Fraud Control Unit, overseeing charities, and pursuing civil enforcement under Executive Law 63(12). Criminal prosecutions generally require a referral or a gubernatorial appointment as special prosecutor, a limit that matters for this campaign.

Letitia James, Democrat, has been attorney general since January 2019, the first woman of color elected to statewide office in New York, the first woman to serve as attorney general, and she seeks a third term. Saritha Komatireddy, Republican, is a Brooklyn-born daughter of Indian immigrants, a Harvard College and Harvard Law School graduate who clerked for then-Judge Brett Kavanaugh, served as an AUSA in the Eastern District of New York from 2013 to 2025 in national security and narcotics roles, and was DEA chief of staff in the Biden administration. She is now a partner at Holtzman Vogel and teaches at Columbia Law School; her campaign credits her with helping convict Genaro Garcia Luna on cartel bribery charges. Neither candidate faced a primary; they debated October 8 on NY1 and Spectrum News.

A Siena College survey, September 11 to 17, 2026, of 1,144 likely voters, margin of error plus or minus 3.9 points, put James at 52 percent and Komatireddy at 39 percent. James's favorability was 51 to 40; more than two-thirds of voters had never heard of Komatireddy or could not rate her. James had about 6.2 million dollars cash on hand, including roughly 3.5 million dollars in matching funds; Komatireddy had about 323,000 dollars. Sabato's Crystal Ball rated the race Safe D as of August 21, 2025; New York has about 6 million registered Democrats and 2.8 million Republicans, and no Republican has won statewide since George Pataki in 2002.

James's record cuts both ways, as the Blackwood piece puts it. On the credit side: a 1.1-billion-dollar opioid settlement; the NRA lawsuit filed in 2020, and a February 2024 jury finding of roughly 4.35 million dollars in restitution against former NRA chief Wayne LaPierre, upheld as of June 3, 2026. Her signature case against Donald Trump, filed in September 2022, produced a judgment of roughly 355 million dollars growing past 464 million dollars with interest, but an Appellate Division panel on August 21, 2025 vacated the monetary penalty, upheld the underlying finding, and left an officer ban and independent monitor in place; James is appealing and the case is unresolved.

On the indictment: a federal grand jury in Virginia indicted James in October 2025 on bank fraud and false statement charges tied to a 2020 Norfolk home purchase; James denied the charges. Judge Cameron Currie dismissed the indictment on November 24, 2025 on appointment law grounds. Two separate grand juries declined to re-indict her in December 2025, the Justice Department appealed, the Fourth Circuit heard arguments September 15, 2026, and no ruling had been issued as of mid-October 2026. The underlying mortgage allegations have never been adjudicated, and James denies them.

On the Medicaid fraud unit: the Empire Center for Public Policy, using federal data, found investigations dropped roughly 50 percent in New York from 2020 to 2025 against a national decline of 19 percent; New York ranked 49th in investigations per billion dollars of Medicaid spending, 51st in indictments and convictions per billion, 49th in criminal recoveries, and 16th in civil recoveries. The office said 69 of its 115 convictions were high-impact; Bill Hammond of the Empire Center called that defense misleading. Komatireddy, citing the office's own annual reports, puts unit recoveries at 168 million dollars in 2019 falling to 31 million dollars in 2024; those figures are her reading of the reports.

Komatireddy has pledged prosecution of hate crimes, repeat offenders, and homeless encampments, and a civil commitment office for the severely mentally ill, though the office cannot do most of that without a referral, a gubernatorial appointment, or new legislation, and her campaign has not detailed which mechanism applies to each pledge, per the Blackwood article. She has also pledged to freeze what she calls a 3-billion-dollar litigation windfall and direct it toward free residential treatment; no independent accounting of that figure was found, and it is her figure and her pledge.

Early voting runs Saturday, October 24 through Sunday, November 1. Registration closes Saturday, October 24, which is also Golden Day, when an unregistered voter can register and vote the same day. Our second story is the City of Poughkeepsie. First, a distinction from what we covered two editions ago. In Ep34, we reported on Dutchess County: the county legislature voting unanimously to physically cover the county's own remaining Flock cameras, with the county executive letting that become law without her signature. This is different. This is the City of Poughkeepsie, a separate jurisdiction, voting to end the city's contracts entirely.

At its October 20 meeting, the Common Council passed a resolution 8 to 1 to end the city's relationship with Flock Safety. Per the Poughkeepsie Journal, read in full for this edition, the resolution directs permanent termination of Flock contracts ideally within four weeks, immediate suspension of camera and database use, a two-week window for Flock to remove its 13 readers or the city removes them and invoices Flock, and no new license plate reader contract without a public hearing. The Flock contract runs through January 2027.

On October 21, Mayor Yvonne Flowers said she intends to veto, citing insufficient prior discussion, investigative leads the cameras have produced, likely loss of gunshot detection through the same provider, and speed and red light cameras planned for early 2027. Those are her stated concerns as reported by the Journal.

Councilmember Nedra Patterson Thompson said the council is prepared to discuss overriding the veto if necessary; the source does not state an override threshold. Organizations including the Mid-Hudson Valley DSA, For the Many, and Poughkeepsie Indivisible attended in support. The council's next meeting is at 6:30 in the evening on November 3.

We close with Anthropic, and again a distinction from the previous edition. In Ep35, we aired Anthropic drafting a proposed frontier AI safety bill. This is different. This is the same company launching a consumer product the same week.

Anthropic launched Claude Duet in research preview on October 21, 2026. Per Anthropic's product page and follow-up coverage, Duet is a proactive agent powered by Claude Opus 5.5 that sends a morning brief, monitors connected apps during the day, takes on assigned tasks, and keeps working on its own cloud computer after the app closes. Available on iOS, Android, and at claude.ai, it asks for approval before sending messages, making payments, signing documents, or deleting anything. Every capability is attributed to Anthropic or the reporting; independent long-term testing does not yet exist.

Access is limited to Claude Pro and Max subscribers in the United States through a waitlist; Teams and Enterprise are not included. There is no separate charge during the preview. Pro costs 20 dollars a month, or 17 dollars a month billed annually; Max starts at 100 dollars a month. Background tasks count against usage limits and Anthropic warns they use those limits faster than chat. CNBC and other coverage framed the launch against competitors including Meta and OpenAI; that framing comes from the coverage, not from Anthropic naming rivals.
