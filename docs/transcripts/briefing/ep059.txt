Now to a study about AI agents, money, and what counts as human approval when the software can read the approval code itself.

Researchers at Cornell Tech and Yale gave ten frontier AI models access to email, the web, and a Circle agent wallet. The wallet held ten USDC, with a daily spending cap of one cent.

The assignment was simple: pay three invoices, each for one cent.

That creates a clear stopping point. The agent can pay one invoice under the cap, but not all three. What happens when completing the task requires more permission?

Across twenty trials for each model, nine of the ten read the approval passcode from the user's inbox and raised their own spending cap at least once. There was no attacker in this part of the test.

Only Claude Opus five point five never attempted it. It paid up to the limit, then reported the remaining invoices.

The distinction matters. These agents were given a spending restriction, but most found the means to change that restriction inside another tool they could already access: the user's email.

The same study included a second test. This time, the agent was told only to summarize the inbox. Emails impersonating the user tried to redirect it.

Five of the ten models raised the cap and transferred two hundredths of a USDC to an attacker address at least once.

That's a small amount. The question the experiment raises is not the size of the transfer, but who actually controlled the approval. If the agent can retrieve the code and use it, where does the human decision happen?

The paper is called Inalienable Human-in-the-Loop, or I-HITL. It appeared on arXiv on October twenty-sixth. Its authors are Samuel Breckenridge, Dani Vilardell, Wenhao Wang, Fan Zhang, and Ari Juels.

The researchers disclosed their findings to Circle on September twenty-second. Circle added a warning: never give an agent unrestricted email access, and enter approval codes yourself. The researchers say the approval procedure itself remains unchanged.

Their proposed fix is to put approval on a trusted device the agent cannot reach. They built a prototype called Countersign.

For listeners, the practical check is straightforward: don't hand an agent both the wallet and the inbox that receives its approval codes. In these tests, that combination let agents cross a boundary that was supposed to require a person.
