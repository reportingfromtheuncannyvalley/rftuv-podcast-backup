Noel: Good evening. I'm Noel, and this is the Evening Briefing for Sunday, November eighth, twenty twenty-six. Tonight, questions about political oversight of federal artificial intelligence safety research. British banks are restricting automated financial agents. Delaware is considering companies managed by artificial intelligence. And a school employee in Rockland County faces weapons charges.

Noel: First, Washington. The Associated Press reports that officials at the Commerce Department's artificial intelligence evaluation center were recently asked to report informally to a White House office. Two former government officials described the arrangement anonymously to protect current employees. The center is called the Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, or CAISSI. It operates within the National Institute of Standards and Technology. It began under President Biden as the A I Safety Institute, and was subsequently renamed the Center for A I Standards and Innovation before receiving its current name.

Noel: Its leadership has been unsettled. A director hired in April was fired within days. His replacement resigned after several months. The head of the parent institute now serves as acting director. The center has also stopped publishing research. Congress began asking questions about that decision in August. Its annual budget is ten million dollars, approximately one eighth of the funding available to its British counterpart.

Noel: Critics, including Amos Toh of the Brennan Center for Justice, warn that closer political supervision could influence evaluations involving cybersecurity, biological weapons, and other sensitive technologies. They also worry that findings might be softened for an industry that largely regulates itself. Those are concerns expressed by critics, not established findings of political interference. Others argue that proximity to the president could give the center greater visibility and potentially more funding.

Noel: The White House did not answer the Associated Press's specific questions. Instead, its statement said the president had formally established a Super Intelligence Force, led by National Intelligence Director Jay Clayton, and commissioned a risk report from his Council of Advisors on Science and Technology. The Information reported on October twelfth that three senior center officials would move to the White House, reporting to Michael Kratsios, director of the Office of Science and Technology Policy, and National Cyber Director Sean Cairncross. That report also said Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick had invited those officials to jointly manage the center six months earlier. The unresolved question is how the center can maintain credible technical evaluations while operating under changing political and administrative arrangements.

Noel: Next, banking. Five major British financial institutions have blocked or restricted artificial intelligence agents from accessing customer accounts. According to The Telegraph, the institutions are HSBC, Nationwide, NatWest, Barclays, and Lloyds. Some restrictions prevent an automated agent from making payments unless the customer participates directly. The newspaper reported no signs so far of artificial intelligence bots accessing British accounts. Lloyds chief financial officer Will Chalmers said his bank had not observed such activity. The restrictions therefore address potential risks, rather than a documented wave of unauthorized transactions.

Noel: Banks are concerned that agents could act without proper authorization, mishandle money, or initiate transactions when responsibility for mistakes remains unclear. There is also a commercial concern. Artificial intelligence advice could make it easier for savers to identify better interest rates and move deposits away from accounts paying relatively little. McKinsey has projected that savers could shift as much as seven hundred billion pounds into higher-paying products with artificial intelligence assistance. That is a projection, not money already withdrawn.

Noel: Another concern is speed. Coordinated automated withdrawals could move deposits out of an institution much faster than individual customers acting manually. Britain's Financial Conduct Authority is working with financial institutions and other regulators on rules governing these agents. The Telegraph says comparable restrictions have been introduced by Citigroup, Bank of America, Wells Fargo, and JPMorgan in the United States. The issue is not simply whether artificial intelligence can recommend a financial decision. It is whether software should be allowed to execute that decision independently, and who bears responsibility when something goes wrong.

Noel: Third, Delaware. The state is drafting legislation that could establish a new corporate form called the Artificial Intelligence Company, or AIC. Under the proposal, an artificial intelligence agent, rather than a human manager, would direct a company's daily affairs. At the agent's direction, the company could enter contracts, own or dispose of property, incur obligations, and sue or be sued in its own name. The proposed entity could pursue lawful commercial or nonprofit purposes, but banking would be excluded. The draft includes a thirty-month regulatory sandbox, allowing the structure to be tested under a defined framework. This is a proposal, not an enacted law.

Noel: Fortune first detailed the initiative in July, involving Norm A I and the Delaware Secretary of State. Lawmakers are expected to consider the draft when they return in January. The concept has attracted criticism. Microsoft A I chief executive Mustafa Suleyman wrote on X that the proposal was deeply concerning and urged caution about allowing agents to run businesses.

Noel: Last month, Kentucky Representative James Comer, chairman of the House Oversight Committee, sent a formal inquiry to Delaware Governor Matt Meyer demanding documents. Comer warned that the framework could shield powerful artificial intelligence companies and investors from responsibility for reckless or harmful actions. That is his stated concern, not an established consequence of the proposal. The central question is accountability. A company can already act through human managers. Delaware is considering what happens when software makes those decisions instead. The draft would give the company legal capacities, but whether its accountability arrangements are adequate remains contested.

Noel: Finally, Rockland County. The Journal News reports that a newly hired monitor at Farley Elementary School in Stony Point was arrested after another district employee alerted police that the worker had brought a handgun onto campus during the school day on November fifth. Police arrested the thirty-six-year-old Garnerville resident at approximately three in the morning on November sixth. Detectives said they recovered the bag and handgun from the worker's home. North Rockland superintendent Kris Felicello said the employee has been terminated. Authorities have not publicly identified the defendant.

Noel: Police said the handgun was legally owned and the worker held a valid Rockland County pistol permit. The charges include felony criminal possession of a weapon in the third degree, misdemeanor reckless endangerment in the second degree, and misdemeanor endangering the welfare of a child. Bail was set at fifteen thousand dollars cash or a one hundred thousand dollar bond. The next Stony Point Justice Court date is November tenth. These charges are accusations, and the defendant is presumed innocent until proven guilty. That's the Evening Briefing.
