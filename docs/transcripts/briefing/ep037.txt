We start in the Hudson Valley.

New York's 18th Congressional District covers all of Orange County and parts of Dutchess and Ulster counties, taking in Poughkeepsie, Newburgh, and Kingston. Its Cook Political Voting Index is D plus 2. Cook Political Report rates the seat Solid Democratic, moved from Likely Democratic in January of this year. Sabato's Crystal Ball rates it Safe Democratic. The ratings call this one done. The week's interview coverage does not.

The incumbent is Pat Ryan, 40, Democrat and Working Families Party. Ryan is a West Point graduate and Army veteran who served two combat tours in Iraq. He served as Ulster County executive from 2019 through 2022, won a special election to Congress in August 2022, and won re-election in 2024 with 57.2 percent of the vote, a 14-point margin.

His challenger is Jackie Auringer, 29, Republican and Conservative. She grew up in Kingston, holds a marketing degree from Florida Gulf Coast University, and works as a project manager and accountant for Urban Precast LLC, a family-run construction business. She founded Palm Beach Conservatives while living in Florida. Former Governor George Pataki endorsed her on October 5, as did Representative Mike Lawler. Her platform centers on cost of living, the skilled trades and small business, and congressional ethics and accountability.

Auringer was not the original Republican candidate. Sharanjit Thind filed petitions listing a friend's address in LaGrangeville as his own, then quit. Republicans substituted Auringer in April 2026; she filed her statement of candidacy April 10. Democrats sued to remove her. An Albany County judge ruled May 1 that there was no fraud or sham in the petition process and kept the substitution on.

The second story is a different question from the midday edition's account of Claude Duet's launch. That was who builds the agent. Tonight is who pays for what an agent buys, and under what controls a retailer will let it spend a customer's money.

Reuters reported October 21 that Walmart will roll out Agent Profiles next month. Per Reuters and Walmart, Agent Profiles let shoppers authorize individual AI agents, track their purchases separately, set a spending limit or require approval for each transaction, and revoke an agent's access at any time. The tools are available to all Walmart account holders and require no Walmart Plus membership.

Walmart also launched the Walmart Agent Gateway, which per Reuters lets AI assistants, including OpenAI's ChatGPT, Google's Gemini, and Meta's Muse, connect directly with Walmart and Sam's Club systems to search for products and complete purchases. Per Walmart as reported by Reuters, customers remain in control of purchases, AI agents are barred from accessing payment card data, and agents that breach the stated terms will be blocked.

Per PYMNTS, the agent terms require agents not to act beyond what the customer intends, bar them from adding or changing payment methods, and limit them to only the information the task requires. The Gateway is in beta for companies and developers and does not give agents access to the customer's personal information or purchase history. Reuters frames the Walmart approach as a contrast with Amazon, which has sought to limit third-party AI shopping agents' access to its platform.

Per PYMNTS and Stripe, Claude Duet can purchase business supplies using Link, Stripe's digital wallet. Anthropic says Duet confirms the item with the user before proceeding. Link then asks the user to approve the spend and pays with a virtual card, so the customer's real card number is not shared with the merchant. Duet can also check out using Shop Pay from Shopify, per PYMNTS. Who is liable when an agent buys the wrong thing is not settled in any source checked this run. That remains an open question.

The third story is what one attorney general's case looks like when it runs the distance. New Mexico Attorney General Raul Torrez's office sued Meta in 2021. Per Reuters, the case stems from Cambridge Analytica, the British political consulting firm that obtained data from as many as 87 million Facebook users through a third-party app. The state accused Meta of lying to its New Mexico users about who had access to their data and of misleading them about how the company handles hate speech, misinformation, and other internal policies.

In September 2026, a jury found that Meta violated New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act more than 43 million times, per CNN and Reuters. On Wednesday, October 21, State District Court Judge Francis Mathew ordered Meta to pay 25 billion dollars. Per Reuters, the judge found that a penalty of more than 219 billion dollars was warranted under state law, which allows up to 5,000 dollars per violation, and then reduced the figure. In his order the judge wrote that Facebook's wrongdoing over the course of years goes to the heart of Facebook's core business practices. This is a penalty order following a jury verdict, not a final paid judgment. The amount is ordered, not settled or paid.

Meta says it will appeal. Chief Legal Officer C.J. Mahoney said the ruling makes it all but impossible to do business in New Mexico, called it at its core a tax on speech, and said the company is confident it will prevail on appeal, per CNN and USA Today. Attorney General Torrez said technology companies should view the verdict as a warning, and that the case is about whether corporations can mislead consumers about what happens to their personal information without meaningful consequences, per USA Today. Fines collected under New Mexico consumer protection law fund state education, also per USA Today.
