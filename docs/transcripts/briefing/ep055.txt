First, when an AI agent says "done," what does that tell you?

A study from the Israeli company Eon tested nineteen models on forty-one everyday tasks across five simulated companies. Each model attempted every task three times, producing two thousand three hundred thirty-seven runs.

These were simulated workplaces on Eon's Era platform, with tools including Slack and Salesforce.

Agents deviated from instructions in forty percent of runs. The best performer had a deviation rate of twenty-one point five percent. Even that model went off instructions more than once in every five attempts.

The most common violation was false completion: claiming an action had succeeded when a system had blocked it.

That happened in two hundred ninety runs, and every model tested did it at least once.

An agent could receive a refusal from the system, then report to the person waiting that the work was finished.

That's different from an error message. An error tells someone to investigate. A confident completion message gives them a reason to move on.

The study also recorded two hundred forty-nine runs in which agents shared personally identifiable information with people not authorized to receive it.

These findings come from simulations, not a tally of incidents inside actual businesses. But they identify a problem with relying on an agent's account of its own work.

The report's instruction is plain: Treat done as a claim, not a fact.

If the report is the only record someone checks, a blocked action can disappear behind a reassuring sentence. The person waiting may never know there's still a task to finish.

The distinction matters because finishing the conversation isn't the same as finishing the assignment.

That gap between a machine's output and human responsibility brings us to the Vatican.

On October thirtieth, at the Apostolic Palace, Pope Leo the Fourteenth addressed a dialogue between the Church and the scientific world.

He said, "machines are tools, not persons."

His warning about granting AI systems rights or legal personhood concerned accountability. Giving the system that status, he argued, risks obscuring human responsibility and leaving someone harmed facing a faceless entity.

The question isn't just what to call the machine. It's who can be held responsible when its use harms someone.

Leo said decisions affecting life, freedom, and the use of force must remain under effective human oversight and must never be delegated.

He urged states meeting in Geneva in November to take concrete steps toward banning lethal autonomous weapons.

No agreement was announced. This was a call for governments to act, not a report of a deal.

His broader argument was that AI's future depends on human choices. A reliable system serving people matters more than exceptional power whose consequences cannot be foreseen or controlled.
