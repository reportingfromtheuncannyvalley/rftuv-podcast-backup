Good afternoon from the Hudson Valley. It is Wednesday, October 21. Three days before early voting opens in New York, there is a statewide race most voters cannot assess, a Dutchess County legislature that voted unanimously to physically cover its own surveillance cameras, and a 150-year-old children's home in Kingston that has reportedly closed with more questions than answers. I'm Noel.

The New York state comptroller is the state's independently elected chief financial officer, chief auditor, and sole trustee of the New York State Common Retirement Fund. As of June 30, 2026, that fund held an estimated 309.7 billion dollars. In the fiscal year ended March 31, 2026, it paid 16.8 billion dollars in benefits to 535,427 annuitants. The office also holds 21 billion dollars in unclaimed funds. The job pays 220,000 dollars a year on a four-year term.

The race to hold it is the least watched on the ballot.

Thomas DiNapoli, 72, Democrat and Working Families Party, has held the office since February 2007. Voters elected him in 2010, 2014, 2018, and 2022, most recently by 57 to 42 percent. He seeks a fifth full term. His challenger is Joseph Hernandez, 53, Republican and Conservative Party. Hernandez came to the United States from Cuba in 1980 at age seven, holds degrees from the University of Florida, Yale, and Oxford, has spent more than 25 years in biotechnology and healthcare, and has never held public office. A Siena poll conducted September 11 to 17, 2026 put DiNapoli ahead 56 to 33. Forty percent of voters said they did not know enough to rate DiNapoli after 19 years in office. Seventy-one percent said the same about Hernandez.

The fund returned an estimated 11.94 percent in the fiscal year ended March 31, 2026.

Hernandez's sharpest campaign promise is to return the 21 billion dollars in unclaimed funds to its owners within his first year. Three things travel with that pledge. First, DiNapoli's office says privacy laws bar it from using tax return data to locate owners. Second, Hernandez's own campaign plan acknowledges that statutory authority may be needed, meaning the first-year timeline depends on the Legislature changing the law. Third, a significant portion of those funds belong to owners who are deceased, dissolved, or unlocatable by means currently available.

On fees: Hernandez claims, as reported by the Legislative Gazette, that outside investment management fees rose from 174 million dollars when DiNapoli took office to 862 million dollars in 2026, an almost 400 percent increase. Those figures are Hernandez's claims. Set against the fund's growth from well under 200 billion dollars to approximately 310 billion dollars over the same period, fees as a share of assets rose far less than the raw totals suggest.

The comptroller is on the ballot Tuesday, November 3, 2026, polls open 6 in the morning to 9 at night. Early voting runs Saturday, October 24 through Sunday, November 1. Registration closes October 24. That date is also Golden Day, when an unregistered voter can register and vote the same day.

On October 13, the Dutchess County Legislature voted unanimously to direct that any county Flock Safety license plate reader still installed on November 1 be physically covered with opaque, non-damaging material. The resolution was introduced by Legislator Chris Drago, a Democrat from Red Hook. It applies only to the 65 cameras the county owns. Another 372 cameras sit on private land or on roads controlled by the state and municipalities, and this resolution does not reach them. Covering here means physically covering the cameras so the public can see they are not in use. It has nothing to do with paying for them.

The county's contract with Flock expired in August. County Executive Sue Serino said she would not renew it.

On October 15, Serino let the measure become law without her signature. In her words, the proper steps to remove these cameras are already underway. The county attorney issued a demand notice on September 30 telling Flock to remove its hardware by October 30. For that reason, Serino said, the resolution unnecessarily exposes the county to potential liability resulting from equipment damage during removal. Her objection is about liability and timing, not support for the cameras.

Two deadlines now sit two days apart: Flock must remove hardware by October 30, and the Legislature's resolution requires covering by November 1.

The last story comes with limits on what we can say, and those limits are part of the story.

On Tuesday, Hudson Valley One reported that questions are swirling around the unexpected closure of the Children's Home of Kingston following a state audit. The home has operated for 150 years at 26 Grove Street in Kingston, serving youth ages 10 to 21, most with a history of trauma and emotional or behavioral challenges. Hudson Valley One's full report was not available for this broadcast.

The state comptroller's office verified audit number 2025-S-7. For the three fiscal years ended June 30, 2022, the home reported about 6,289,798 dollars in reimbursable costs. Auditors identified 670,549 dollars in costs that did not comply with state rules. The four largest pieces: 201,518 dollars in costs that lacked required records or invoices; 198,335 dollars for one-to-one aides charged to the wrong program; 118,788 dollars in non-program personnel costs; and 107,236 dollars in overallocated facility-related expenses.

The audit does not order a closure. Nothing verified for this broadcast establishes that the audit caused the closure. Those are two separate facts, and the connection between them has not been established.

What remains unknown and must be named: the closure timeline, who was informed and when, how many residents and staff are affected, where the youth the home served will go, and any statement from the home or from a state agency.
