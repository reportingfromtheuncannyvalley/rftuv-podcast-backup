Tonight's final story moves to Washington. The White House AI safety accord covered in earlier reporting was voluntary, a commitment by the labs to police themselves. Politico's October 16 report is different: Anthropic has privately pitched lawmakers a draft bill on frontier AI safety. Per Politico, the draft is confidential and unpublished and has not been introduced in Congress. A company spokesperson said, per follow-up coverage, that the text converts Anthropic's Advanced AI Framework into statutory language.

Per Politico's reporting, the draft would let the government block a model only when facing a foreseeable and imminent risk of more than one billion dollars in damage, or more than 50 people killed or seriously injured, from one of three situations: the creation or release of a biological weapon, a model slipping out of its developer's control, or a cyberattack. Below that threshold, the power to suspend a model would not apply.

Only the Secretary of Commerce could seek a blocking order, from a federal judge in Washington, D.C. An order would last up to 45 days, and the government would have to show no less restrictive measure would achieve the same result. Independent evaluators registered with the Commerce Department would assess covered developers every 180 days and publish findings, with a duty to notify Commerce within 72 hours of identifying any listed risk. The draft would also, per Politico, preempt some state laws on AI disclosure, evaluation, and incident reporting.

Follow-up reporting notes that OpenAI supports the proposal. Safety groups and some Democratic lawmakers see it as locking in favorable terms while the industry still controls the definitions. Supporters say the threshold must be high enough to hold up in court. Critics say that by the time harm is foreseeable at the scale of 50 deaths, the window to prevent it has typically already closed. No congressional sponsor had been identified in the sourcing reviewed.

From a voluntary commitment to a proposed statute with a numeric trigger, drafted by the company that would be regulated under it. Who sets the threshold, who proves it in court, and why the regulated company holds the pen are questions the reporting raises and leaves open.
