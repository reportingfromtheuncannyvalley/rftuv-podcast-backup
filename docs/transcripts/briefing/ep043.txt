Early voting is in its second day in New York. Tonight we cover ballot entries that do not always make the headlines, starting with the governor's race.

The printed ballot offers two tickets. Kathy Hochul and Adrienne Adams appear on the Democratic and Working Families lines. Bruce Blakeman and Todd Hood appear on the Republican, Conservative, and Vote Affordable lines. Beyond those tickets, the race includes two declared write-in candidates and one disqualified candidacy, none of which appears in any county.

Jean Anglade is running as an independent write-in on a platform of government accountability, affordability, and public safety. No independent biographical reporting on Anglade appeared in any reliable outlet.

Jonathan Makeley is running as a write-in for the Prohibition Party. He is a historian from Angelica, in Western New York, and serves as party chairman for New York. Ballot-access thresholds make a party line impractical. His platform includes treating alcohol, tobacco, and drugs as public-health problems, strengthening ethics laws, and expanding ballot access. The modern Prohibition Party advocates heavy regulation of alcohol, not a ban.

The third candidacy ended before the ballot was printed. Larry Sharpe sought the ballot through an independent nominating petition under the Coalition party name, with Shannon Joy Bones as his running mate. The State Board of Elections ruled his petition invalid in June twenty twenty six: it contained no more than one thousand six hundred fifteen possible signatures against the forty five thousand required, a shortfall of more than forty three thousand. Afterward, the Libertarian Party of New York launched Vote NOTA, None of the Above, protesting the ballot-access rules.

The law changed in twenty twenty. A party seeking recognized status must now receive the greater of one hundred thirty thousand votes or two percent in statewide elections, up from fifty thousand votes, and the petition threshold for independents tripled from fifteen thousand to forty five thousand. Since then, only the Working Families and Conservative parties have retained recognized status alongside the two major parties.

On write-in mechanics: fill in the bubble or box and write the candidate's name. New York counts write-in votes only for candidates with declared status. Both Anglade and Makeley have declared, so their votes will be canvassed. A write-in for an undeclared name is set aside as informal. Early voting runs through November first, Election Day is November third, polls open six A M to nine P M, and the Dutchess County voting guide is in the show notes.

We turn now to Senate District thirty nine. The Poughkeepsie Journal calls it possibly the most competitive, high-stakes election in the mid-Hudson Valley, covering Beacon, Poughkeepsie, and Newburgh, and towns across Dutchess, Orange, and Putnam counties. Voters choose November third between incumbent Rob Rolison and challenger Lisa Kaul.

Rolison served as a Town of Poughkeepsie police officer for twenty six years, county legislator for twelve, and mayor of Poughkeepsie from twenty sixteen to twenty twenty two, and has held the Senate seat since twenty twenty two. Kaul has served as a county legislator since twenty twenty three, is a cultural anthropologist who has worked at Vassar College, and told the Journal she is running because business as usual is not working. Both name affordability as their top issue.

On utilities, their approaches split. Rolison would reintroduce Senate Bill S8463, the Utility Tax Holiday bill, exempting utility bills from state taxes and surcharges for one year, with two years of exemptions from green energy mandated tariffs, and would open a satellite office in Newburgh two to three days a week. Kaul calls Central Hudson a state-sanctioned monopoly and supports a Hudson Valley Power Authority, a public corporation that would acquire and operate Central Hudson.

On housing and public safety, Kaul treats housing as a public safety concern. She would protect renters, fund accessory dwelling units through the Plus One ADU Program, and redevelop vacant spaces. Rolison says each community is different and that public safety should be driven by local professionals rather than Albany.

Each told the Journal how they want to be judged. Kaul says judge her by whether rents and utility bills go down, healthcare becomes accessible, and transit is reliable. Rolison says he hopes people will say he was there for everyone and always tried to come from the right place.

For our final story, New York is easing part of its ban on Native American imagery in public schools. The important word is portion. The state plans to repeal the section of its twenty twenty three rule barring school officers and employees from utilizing or promoting any Indigenous name, logo, or mascot on campus or at a school function. The State Education Department will recommend leaving those decisions to local districts, and spokesperson JP O'Hare confirmed that recommendation, according to the reporting.

What does not change is just as important. The core twenty twenty three rule barring public schools from using Indigenous names, logos, and mascots stands, and nothing here restores a school's own mascot. The Regents are expected to announce the partial repeal at a November meeting, with a final vote in February after a public comment period. Oral arguments are set for November fourth. Members of the state's Indigenous Mascot Advisory Council have called the partial rollback inconsistent and unacceptable.

Massapequa High School on Long Island, whose teams are the Chiefs, is at the center of this fight. The district fought the ban for years, sought support from President Trump, and estimated a full rebrand at nearly one million dollars. Board of Education President Kerry Wachter said the district was proud of this victory and urged the Regents to repeal the regulation in its entirety, adding, "Once a Chief, always a Chief," according to the reporting. The state's change covers the employee clause only, and the school mascot ban stands.
