First, Red Hook.

For the whole month of November, every fruit, every vegetable, and every protein on the lunch trays in the Red Hook Central School District comes from New York, sourced from New York producers, several right here in the Hudson Valley. This is the third year in a row Red Hook has done it. The district calls it New York November.

Created by former Food Service Director Larry Anthony, the program is now run by his successor, Sam Pride.

The month's landmarks, stated once. The kickoff is this Thursday, November fifth, with a menu designed by culinary students from the Culinary Institute of America in Hyde Park, who spent October shadowing the district's kitchen staff. Every Friday brings New York Pizza Palooza, built from the week's remaining New York ingredients. On November nineteenth, the kitchen staff will roast pork in house for the first time. And the month closes on Tuesday, November twenty-fourth, with a Thanksgiving meal of turkey, garlic smashed potatoes, and cherry crisp.

Behind it sits a state incentive: buy at least thirty percent of lunch ingredients from New York farms, and reimbursement rises from five point nine cents a meal to twenty-five cents. Red Hook, by the district's account, passes that threshold by a wide margin.

Most districts do not. A state comptroller audit found that only seventy-three of nearly seven hundred sixty-two eligible school food authorities outside New York City took part in the twenty twenty-four to twenty-five school year. What Red Hook solved is the logistics: sourcing at this level, at school lunch prices, without breaking the budget. Three Novembers in, it looks less like a promotion and more like a habit.

Now to the cameras.

La Voce di New York reported on Saturday that Flock cameras are still up in Dutchess County, despite a pledge to remove them within thirty days. That is the report, stated as the report. Here is the background it lands on.

The county ran sixty-five Flock license plate readers. The contract expired at the end of August. On September twenty-fifth, District Attorney Anthony Parisi and Sheriff Kirk Imperati withdrew their support, and the cameras were deactivated that day. The county attorney then demanded Flock remove all its hardware from county property by October thirtieth.

The Legislature added a backstop, unanimously. Any county camera still standing on November first, today, must be covered with opaque material that does not damage it, and stay covered until it comes down.

So today is the test date. If county cameras are still on their poles this morning, the county's own resolution says they should be covered.

One distinction matters, and it is easy to lose. The resolution covers only the county's sixty-five cameras. Another three hundred seventy-two license plate readers operate in Dutchess County on private land and on state and town roads, and the resolution does not reach them. A camera still standing on a town road is not, by itself, proof the county missed its deadline, and which cameras any given report counted is the question to ask first. What is solid today is narrow: the cover requirement is now in effect, and whether the county's cameras are covered is something any driver here can see.

Next, a sentence in White Plains.

On Wednesday, a federal judge sentenced Michael Vasconcellos, forty-five, of Mahopac, to thirty months in prison for his role in fabricating gas pipeline safety test results. He will also serve three years of supervised release, and he was ordered to pay more than sixty-four million dollars in restitution. He pleaded guilty in April to conspiring to commit wire fraud.

According to the United States Attorney, between about twenty sixteen and twenty twenty-three, Vasconcellos and a second inspector, Liam Treibert, were hired to test welds on natural gas pipelines being installed across New York City and Westchester County. The utility is Con Edison, according to the Journal News. The tests are x-rays of the welds, done because a bad weld can leak, and a leak can explode.

Prosecutors say they lied about inspecting hundreds of welds they never examined, and created false records to cover it. The practice has a name in the trade: radaring. You x-ray one weld twice, and pass the second set of images off as a different weld nobody ever looked at.

Because Con Edison could not tell which welds had really been checked, it has had to dig up pipeline and test the welds again, at a cost of more than sixty-four million dollars so far.

United States Attorney Jamie McDonald put the harm in one sentence: New Yorkers were left relying on critical safety checks that never happened. Treibert's case is still pending, according to the Journal News, and nothing beyond that is claimed about him here.

A missed inspection can be rescheduled. A faked one cannot, because nobody knows it is missing. That is why the repair bill runs to tens of millions.
