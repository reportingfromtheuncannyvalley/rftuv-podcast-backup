New York State Police begin a five-day Move Over Law enforcement campaign today, running through Friday, November sixth. It's part of Crash Responder Safety Week, in partnership with the state Department of Transportation and the Thruway Authority.

June Mercer's reporting in our RFTUV blotter lays out the important distinction. This law isn't only about a police car with its lights on, or a tow truck helping someone beside the highway.

An expansion that took effect on March twenty-seventh, twenty twenty-four, extended the law to any vehicle parked, stopped, or standing on the shoulder or elsewhere on a parkway or controlled-access highway.

That includes a disabled car with a flat tire or mechanical trouble. There does not have to be an emergency responder present.

Picture the difference. A driver beside a car with a flat tire is covered, not just the people who may arrive to help. The law protects emergency responders, road crews, tow truck operators, and ordinary people stopped along the road.

The practical guidance is straightforward: slow down and move over a lane when it's safe. If a lane change isn't safe, slow down and pass cautiously. Watch both shoulders for flashing lights, reflective clothing, and people working near traffic.

That's the instruction to carry into the week. And the part to remember about the expanded law is the person waiting for help. A stopped car does not need a trooper beside it to fall within the law's protection.

Back here in the Hudson Valley, Bard College in Annandale-on-Hudson has opened the Ralph Ellison Center.

There is a direct connection between the writer and this campus. Ellison taught American and Russian literature at Bard from nineteen fifty-eight to nineteen sixty-one. Now the college is taking on a lasting role in the care and use of his work.

The center was made possible by a three-point-six-million-dollar donation from the Ralph and Fanny Ellison Charitable Trust. Under the agreement, Bard now holds all the intellectual rights, copyrights, and royalties from Ellison's works, including Invisible Man. The trust will wind down and close.

Invisible Man was published in nineteen fifty-two. It was the only novel Ellison published during his lifetime, and it made him the first Black writer to win the National Book Award for Fiction.

Bard's first-year students read the novel today. So this isn't only a connection to someone who taught there decades ago. His writing is already part of what students encounter at the beginning of their time at the college.

There's an important distinction in what has moved to Bard and what has not. The rights and royalties have transferred. Ellison's archive remains at the Library of Congress.

The center plans to digitize that archive so scholars and members of the public can use it more widely. A new center in Annandale does not mean the physical papers have been moved there.

The plans also reach beyond managing the rights. They include the Ralph Ellison Lectures, a biannual gathering, along with artist and writer residencies.

A ten-week student internship combines archival work and independent research, in partnership with historically Black colleges and universities. It began this past summer with six students, including Bard senior Bee Hitz.

And a yearlong commemoration is planned for twenty twenty-seven, the seventy-fifth anniversary of Invisible Man.

The center is directed by Nicholas Alton Lewis, Bard's associate dean. John F. Callahan, the Ellison estate's literary executor, serves as a senior fellow.

Taken together, those plans put students, researchers, artists, and writers into the center's work. The connection to Ellison's years at Bard is part of its history. The internships, digitization, and residencies are how that connection will continue.
