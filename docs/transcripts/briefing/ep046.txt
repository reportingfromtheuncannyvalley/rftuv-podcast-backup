Three stories today. Anthropic files to go public, and the paperwork teaches a reading lesson. A missing teenager is located safe, closing that thread. And the Hudson Valley Train Show comes back to the valley.

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude family of A I models, filed its S-one registration statement Monday morning with the Securities and Exchange Commission. The company is seeking to list its Class A common stock on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker symbol ANT. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the underwriting.

The filing contains no price range and no share count. The registration statement is not yet effective, and no shares can be sold yet. Reuters has reported that I P O expectations put the valuation at around one point five dollarstrillion. The Wall Street Journal has put the figure at about two dollarstrillion. Both of those are external reporting, not numbers that appear in the filing itself.

Here is the reading lesson, and it is worth slowing down for. For the first six months of twenty twenty six, Anthropic reported revenue of sixteen point two nine billion dollars. In the same six months, it reported a net loss of eighty point seven billion dollars. Both numbers are accurate. They are not in contradiction, and understanding why is what a prospective buyer needs to carry into the rest of this story.

About seventy two point four billion dollars of that loss is a non-cash accounting charge. As Anthropic's private valuation climbed in recent quarters, the paper value of its convertible notes and similar instruments climbed with it. Accounting standards require that increase to be recorded as a loss on the income statement, even though no cash left the company for it. The charge is a function of how the instruments were structured, not a sign that the business burned through eighty point seven billion dollars. It resolves when those instruments convert to equity at the I P O.

Strip that charge out and the picture changes. The filing reports adjusted operating income of one point six billion dollars for the first half of the year. It reports positive operating cash flow of three point nine three billion dollars for the same period. That means the company collected more cash than it spent on operations in the first six months of twenty twenty six. The June quarter produced the company's first quarterly operating profit. Those are the numbers that describe whether the business is generating cash. The eighty point seven billion dollars headline loss describes an accounting treatment applied to a rising valuation.

The growth line in the filing is steep enough that it is worth reading slowly. Anthropic brought in forty one million dollars in revenue across all of twenty twenty three. By all of twenty twenty five that number had reached four point five nine billion dollars. In just the first half of twenty twenty six alone it was sixteen point two nine billion dollars. Headcount stood at about five thousand three hundred at the end of September twenty twenty six, up from roughly nine hundred forty at the end of twenty twenty four.

That is the business story. The governance story is the one a buyer cannot diversify away.

Anthropic is a controlled company. A single Class F share holds fifty point one percent of the total stockholder vote, and that share is held by a founders' entity. Separately, a mechanism tied to the Long-Term Benefit Trust allows the trust to elect the directors who make up a majority of the board. A public shareholder who buys ANT buys the economics of Anthropic. They buy almost none of its direction. That is not hidden in the footnotes, it is the structure of the offering, and it is worth naming plainly before the shares ever price. An investor in ANT takes the growth story and the cash flow story with them. The governance structure stays where it is.
