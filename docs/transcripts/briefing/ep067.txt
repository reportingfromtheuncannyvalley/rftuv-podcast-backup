Noel: Midday Briefing, for Sunday, November eighth, twenty twenty-six.

Noel: The Justice Department has dropped its lawsuit seeking to separate certain California ballots before they were counted, after four unsuccessful emergency court requests. Also today, new reporting points to a reset in expectations around Anthropic's potential stock market debut. And in the Hudson Valley, Newburgh has secured state funding for a major sewer replacement project.

Noel: We begin with the California case, where the distinction between an emergency court decision and a final ruling matters.

Noel: The Justice Department voluntarily dismissed its lawsuit on Friday, November sixth, the day after Election Day. The case was filed on October fifteenth, jointly with the Department of Homeland Security. Federal officials alleged that more than twenty thousand likely noncitizens had been mailed ballots in California. They asked a court to require the state to segregate those ballots before tabulation. That was the government's allegation, not a finding that those people had voted illegally, or that the underlying list was accurate.

Noel: The government sought urgent intervention, but four emergency efforts failed. On October twenty-sixth, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals sent the first appeal back to the lower court. On October twenty-eighth, federal Judge David Carter denied a temporary restraining order. On October thirtieth, the Ninth Circuit denied an emergency injunction. And on November second, the Supreme Court refused the government's emergency application. None of those decisions resolved the underlying legal merits. They addressed whether emergency relief should be granted.

Noel: The government then dismissed the case voluntarily, one week before an evidentiary hearing that Judge Carter had ordered to examine the reliability of the government's list. California Attorney General Rob Bonta argued that the timing was significant. He said, quote, Rather than face that scrutiny, it chose to drop its case, end quote. That is Bonta's interpretation of the dismissal. The Justice Department's decision to withdraw the case does not itself establish why it did so.

Noel: But the reliability question was not merely hypothetical. In a separate, related case, a Justice Department attorney acknowledged on the record that the lists used to match possible noncitizens carried a high error rate. That admission raises an important distinction. A name appearing on a matching list is not the same as verified evidence that an ineligible person cast a ballot.

Noel: And the civil lawsuit is not the whole federal enforcement story. On November fourth, grand juries indicted ten noncitizens in Los Angeles and Orange counties. Six more people were charged on November second, and three more were indicted on October twenty-eighth. Those are separate criminal proceedings. Charges and indictments are allegations, not convictions, and they should not be treated as proof that the disputed civil list was accurate. Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin said when the lawsuit was filed that the department's analysis was ongoing. For now, the California ballot segregation case has ended without a ruling on the underlying legal theory. Whether that theory could succeed in another case remains an open question.

Noel: Next, the business of artificial intelligence, where the size of a possible public offering is attracting attention, but the numbers require careful separation. Axios reported Friday, November sixth, that people familiar with Anthropic's offering described a downward reset in expectations for its initial public offering valuation. Those sources were unnamed. The report does not turn their expectations into an official offering price. Anthropic filed its registration statement on October twenty-sixth and amended it on November second. Neither version included a proposed price range. In late October, I T Pro reported that a valuation between one point five trillion and two trillion dollars was under discussion. Again, that was a reported expectation, not a figure established by the filing.

Noel: What the filing does provide is a closer look at how Anthropic earns money and what it spends to operate. Eighty-three percent of revenue in the first half of twenty twenty-six came from consumption-based fees, charged according to the tokens customers use. The company reported net revenue retention of approximately nine hundred percent, a measure indicating substantial expansion in spending among existing customers under the company's reported calculation.

Noel: There is also customer concentration to consider. Two customers each accounted for twelve percent of revenue in twenty twenty-five. For the nine months ending September thirtieth, twenty twenty-six, the five largest customers accounted for about twenty-five percent of revenue. And approximately forty-eight percent of revenue in that same nine-month period was routed through Amazon and Alphabet's Google. That routing figure does not mean those two companies were necessarily the end customers behind every dollar. It describes the channels through which revenue flowed.

Noel: On the cost side, Anthropic reported eight point nine four five billion dollars in compute and infrastructure expenses during the first half of twenty twenty-six. Its infrastructure commitments extend as far as twenty forty-five. The company also said Claude Opus five point five reduced customer costs per task by approximately forty percent compared with Opus five. Those details help explain why valuation discussions are more complicated than a single headline number. Anthropic reports rapid customer spending expansion and lower costs for certain tasks, alongside enormous infrastructure expenses and long-term commitments. The filing gives investors data to evaluate. It does not settle what price the market will ultimately assign.

Noel: Back in the Hudson Valley, the City of Newburgh has received a ten million dollar state award for a major water quality project. The funding, through the Water Quality Improvement Project program, is intended to replace the South Interceptor Sewer with a new fifty-four-inch interceptor. The project would increase full-flow capacity from twenty point nine million gallons per day to fifty-six million gallons per day. The goal is to reduce combined sewer overflows into the Hudson River. The award is part of a thirty point four million dollar round of Hudson Valley water quality funding.

Noel: There is an important distinction between receiving an award and beginning construction. Work has not started. The city has not announced a design contract, construction bids, or a project schedule. So while the funding is a concrete development, residents should not interpret it as a notice that excavation is about to begin.

Noel: And one sports lookahead before we go: Beacon shut out Marlboro, thirty-four to nothing, Friday night, with Elijah Epps rushing for three touchdowns. The Bulldogs face Port Jervis in the Section Nine Class B final next Saturday, November fourteenth. Port Jervis beat Beacon, twenty-four to fourteen, during the regular season.
