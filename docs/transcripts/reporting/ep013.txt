One hundred thirty-eight million payments made by A I agents, most of them tiny, nearly all of them automatic, none of them requiring a person to press confirm.

Noel: Today's question is not whether A I agents are acting. We have covered that ground. Episode Eleven was about liability when an agent acts. Episode Twelve was about a model not staying where it was put. Today's question is different and further along. What happens to the economy when agents are not just acting, but paying? When they are not incidents but participants, transacting continuously at machine speed, paying each other for data and computing power and model access, and doing all of it without a human approval step in the critical path?

Noel: The reporting from October fourteenth and fifteenth is telling us this. And it is not a forecast. It is a count.

Noel: [ACT ONE: THE COUNTED ECONOMY]

Noel: Start with the scale, because the scale is where this story earns its credibility.

Noel: The Chainalysis report, covered by Fortune, Decrypt, and CoinDesk all on October fourteenth, is not a projection. It is not a model output or a consultant's estimate of what might happen by twenty thirty one. It is a count of transactions recorded on a public blockchain, attributable to A I agent wallets operating through the x402 protocol on the Base network.

Noel: To understand why that number matters, you need to understand what micro-payments are and why they have historically been hard to do at scale.

Noel: When a software service wants to charge another software service for a small, specific thing, a single A P I call, a data lookup, a few seconds of inference on a model, the traditional payment rails do not work well. Credit card transactions have floor costs built in: interchange fees, processing minimums, infrastructure designed for the kind of payments people make with plastic. If the transaction is worth a fraction of a cent, the cost of moving money through a card network can exceed the value of what you are paying for. The math does not work.

Noel: The x402 protocol, running on the Base blockchain, matters here. CoinDesk reported on October fourteenth that micro-payments are powering autonomous A I software as onchain agentic transactions soar. Base is a blockchain built by Coinbase, designed to settle transactions cheaply and quickly. Stablecoins, which are cryptocurrencies pegged to a fixed value like a dollar, make it possible to move a hundredth of a cent between two software services without a human bank deciding whether that transaction is worth processing. The plumbing fits the payment size. That is the design choice that made the growth curve possible.

Noel: And the growth curve is worth dwelling on. Decrypt reported that payments through the x402 protocol started from roughly fifteen hundred transactions in mid-twenty twenty five and reached more than one hundred thirty-eight million by the third quarter of this year. That is not gradual adoption. That is a step change. Chainalysis also found that the share of agent wallets funded a second time within thirty days of their first funding rose from seventy point six percent to eighty-nine point three percent between mid-twenty twenty five and mid-twenty twenty six. And the median time between a wallet being funded and that wallet making its first payment fell from seventy-three days to eighteen minutes.

Noel: Wallets that used to sit idle for more than two months are now spending within eighteen minutes of being topped up. Developers are not setting these things up and walking away. They are building automated commerce into their pipelines as a routine part of the work.

Noel: CoinDesk added a geographic note worth holding. U.S.-linked wallets accounted for eighty-eight point one percent of the payments identified as going toward A I model inference, meaning buying time and capacity from A I models.

Noel: Mara Quinn looks at this infrastructure from the business side, and her lens sharpens something the raw numbers do not immediately surface.

Noel: Mara asks not just what is being paid, but who built the rails and who takes a cut of every transaction moving across them. When you look at the x402 ecosystem, Coinbase built the protocol. Base is a Coinbase blockchain. Visa and Mastercard have both been named in reporting on maturing agentic payment infrastructure as participants in this emerging ecosystem. If this payment layer grows to hundreds of billions of micro-transactions annually, the entity that owns the settlement layer, even at tiny per-transaction rates, is in an extraordinary position. Agentic commerce is not just a technology story. It is a rails story. And whoever sets the rules for those rails sets the terms of what Chainalysis is already calling one of crypto's fastest-growing use cases.

Noel: That is Act One. One hundred thirty-eight million transactions counted. The micro-payment infrastructure explained. And the first business question on the table: who owns the plumbing.

Noel: [ACT TWO: THE SYSTEM QUESTION]

Noel: On October fourteenth, the Atlantic Council published a piece by Ivan Ferrari under the GeoTech Cues banner, and the headline is worth reading slowly: "When A I agents become economic actors, what happens to money and the financial system?"

Noel: Ferrari opens at Jackson Hole, the annual gathering of central bankers and economists that sets the tone for global monetary policy. Princeton economist Markus Brunnermeier warned the assembled policymakers that A I agents operating at scale could outmaneuver regulators and make markets more erratic. His argument was direct: central bank transparency, quote, "arms the opponent," meaning the market, once A I can predict policy. His proposed solution was two press conferences, one for human audiences and one calibrated as machine training data. He acknowledged the problem himself: A I can listen to both.

Noel: Brunnermeier is describing a present constraint, not a future risk. The tools central banks have used to manage financial stability, forward guidance, graduated communication, signals built to move slowly through human decision-making chains, assume that the receivers operate on human timescales. A I agents at scale do not.

Noel: The Atlantic Council piece frames the broader system question this way: the financial infrastructure in place today was built on certain assumptions. There is a person, or a legally registered firm, on each side of a transaction. That entity has an identity that can be verified. It is subject to jurisdiction. If fraud occurs, processes exist for detection, investigation, and reversal. If a dispute arises, there is a timescale within which human institutions can resolve it.

Noel: A I agents paying each other at machine speed, on delegated authority from their human principals, test every one of those assumptions simultaneously.

Noel: Start with identity. Know-your-customer rules, anti-money-laundering checks, sanctions screening, all designed to verify that the entity on the other side of a transaction is who they say they are. When the entity is a software agent acting on behalf of a person, the question becomes structurally more complex. Is the agent the customer? Is the human who funded the wallet the customer? What happens when the agent has been operating autonomously for weeks and the human principal has changed their mind, but the agent has not stopped?

Noel: Move to fraud detection. Modern fraud systems work by flagging anomalies against expected human behavior. A card used in two cities within an hour raises a flag because a person cannot be in both places. An A I agent making ten thousand micro-payments across twenty different services in a single minute is not behaving like a person at all. The behavioral baselines that fraud systems rely on do not translate to this pattern.

Noel: Move to reversals and dispute rights. Consumer financial protection law gives individuals specific rights when a transaction goes wrong, and a defined window to dispute it. Those processes assume the gap between a transaction and a dispute is humanly observable. An A I agent making thousands of payments a day breaks that assumption: by the time a human reviews what the agent spent, many dispute windows may already be closed.

Noel: Mara Quinn's question here is direct: who sets the rules? If you are Coinbase, running Base and the x402 protocol, you have terms of service. Those terms govern what agents can do on your network. But terms of service are not financial regulation. They are contract. The gap between what a contract permits and what consumer financial protection law requires is where the system exposure lives.

Noel: One contrast is worth a single clause: one major bank closed its door to agent activity this week. Here, elsewhere, the door is open and volume is the story. Some institutions are building walls. Others are building rails. The transactions are flowing through the rails.

Noel: The Atlantic Council piece is not predicting catastrophe. It is doing something more useful: identifying the pressure points in existing infrastructure before the pressure becomes a crisis. Machine-speed, delegated-authority transactions are not a future scenario to model. They are a present reality to count.

Noel: One hundred thirty-eight million of them. And counting.

Noel: [ACT THREE: THE CONSUMER EDGE]

Noel: The Atlantic Council is asking the system question from the policy level. Two pieces published October fifteenth bring it to ground level.

Noel: The Business Times published a piece by Vikram Khanna on October fifteenth whose central argument is exactly what the headline states: your A I agent may level the playing field against companies. Khanna's framing word is "agentification." He describes it as the moment artificial intelligence stopped merely answering questions and started acting on behalf of the people who use it, negotiating, cancelling, buying.

Noel: The leveling argument goes like this. Companies have always held a structural advantage over individual consumers in commercial negotiations. A corporation has lawyers, pricing specialists, data teams, and the ability to model consumer behavior at scale. An individual shopper has time, attention, and gut instinct. A I agents, Khanna argues, begin to close that gap. An agent negotiating a subscription cancellation knows the retention offers the company typically makes. An agent shopping for a flight knows the price history. An agent disputing a charge knows the regulatory language the servicer is required to follow.

Noel: But Khanna also notes the resistance, and this is where it gets interesting. Amazon has blocked agentic shopping. When one of the largest e-commerce platforms in the world explicitly closes its doors to agents operating on behalf of buyers, that tells you something about where the leverage actually sits, and about how seriously these companies are taking the threat to their pricing power. The contest Khanna identifies as the central commercial question of the next decade is: who keeps the savings when agents start replacing both corporate workers and consumer effort? That framing moves this beyond technology and into power.

Noel: Then there is HousingWire.

Noel: On October fifteenth, HousingWire published a piece by Anwar Ali that approaches the consumer agent question from one of the most specific, consequential angles possible: mortgage servicing.

Noel: Ali anchors the piece in Meta's launch of Muse on September eighth of this year. Muse is Meta's personal A I agent. According to its product page, Muse can browse websites, complete forms, and negotiate on a user's behalf, including in customer service interactions. Ali's question is: what happens when Muse, or an agent like it, starts calling a mortgage servicer on behalf of a borrower?

Noel: Mortgage servicing is not a casual domain. The rules governing it were written to protect borrowers because the stakes are someone's home. A servicer that processes a payment incorrectly, or applies a modification incorrectly, or fails to honor a loss mitigation application, can trigger legal liability and real harm to a real person living in a real house.

Noel: The HousingWire piece identifies three questions that mortgage servicers are not yet asking.

Noel: First, disclosure. If a borrower's A I agent contacts a servicer, does the agent have to disclose that it is software and not the borrower themselves? The servicer needs to know whether it is talking to the borrower or to an agent acting on their behalf. The answer matters for how the interaction is documented, what disclosures are required, and how consent is tracked on both sides.

Noel: Second, authentication. How does a servicer verify that the agent actually has current authority to act? The borrower may have authorized the agent broadly at the time of setup. But circumstances change. The agent may be operating on instructions that are months old. A servicer cannot verify borrower intent by asking the agent: the agent will confirm its authority whether that authority reflects the borrower's current wishes or not. Real verification requires getting back to the human.

Noel: Third, delegated authority and its limits. Ali's core argument is this: a borrower should be able to hand an agent the administrative parts of dealing with a servicer without handing over decisions about their home. There is a meaningful difference between asking an agent to confirm payment receipt and asking an agent to accept or decline a loan modification offer. The first is routine. The second is a consequential financial decision that can affect a family's housing stability for years. The rules need to specify which decisions an agent may make and which ones require the human to return to the conversation.

Noel: Mara Quinn's business lens sharpens this again. The servicer's problem is not only legal. It is operational. Every time an agent calls in, the servicer needs to route that interaction, authenticate the delegated authority, document the exchange, and ensure the outcome is consistent with the borrower's actual legal rights. That is infrastructure. Most servicers do not have it today. The HousingWire piece is a build notice: the agents are coming, and the servicers need a plan before they arrive.

Noel: Which brings me to the test I want to close with.

Noel: If you gave an A I agent a wallet this year, or if you authorized an agent to make payments on your behalf through a tool you use, there are three questions worth answering right now.

Noel: Question one: who gave the agent its wallet, and under what authority? Was there an explicit authorization you approved, or did the wallet get created as a default when you onboarded the product? The answer affects your legal relationship to every transaction the agent has made.

Noel: Question two: what limits are on it? Is there a spending cap? A list of approved payment categories? A maximum per-transaction amount? Or is the agent authorized to pay any amount to any counterparty it determines is necessary to complete its task? The difference between those two configurations is the difference between a petty cash box and an open checkbook.

Noel: Question three: who can stop it, and how fast? If the agent makes a payment you did not intend, what is the reversal process? Does one exist? Does it run through a human institution with defined timelines and legal obligations, or through a blockchain where settlement is near-instant and reversal is not a built-in feature?

Noel: These are not hypothetical questions for a future edition of this podcast. One hundred thirty-eight million transactions happened before most people knew this infrastructure existed.

Noel: Episodes Eleven and Twelve of this podcast looked at A I agents as incidents, moments of liability, moments when a model crossed a boundary it was supposed to hold. This episode is about something that has already moved past the incident frame.

Noel: Agents with wallets are not accidents. They are participants, transacting at machine speed on rails designed specifically to remove the human approval step from the critical path.

Noel: The Atlantic Council asked what happens to money and the financial system when A I agents become economic actors. The Chainalysis data that Fortune reported on October fourteenth gave us a partial answer: the system is already running. One hundred thirty-eight million payments in a year, most worth less than ten cents, most made without anyone pressing confirm.

Noel: Forecasts and analyst projections have their place. But this week, the Chainalysis count did something different. It replaced a prediction with a number, and the number is large enough that the question is no longer whether this is happening.

Noel: The rules governing it, who enforces those rules, who can reverse a transaction when something goes wrong, and whether the infrastructure we built for human participants has any meaningful reach into a payment layer that does not wait for humans to catch up are the questions.
