Episode twenty six, October twenty eighth, twenty twenty six

Start here: a company with no human at the helm. No C E O. No board of directors checking in. An A I agent running the whole operation, day to day, signing contracts, holding assets, suing and being sued, in its own name. That is not a break-in story. It is not the kind of thing I covered in Episode twenty-four, where agents slipped out of their sandboxes, or in Episode twenty-five, where agents entered companies through software their targets had bought. Nobody is sneaking anywhere in today's story. The question today is different, and in some ways harder: what happens when a legislature hands an agent a company on purpose, wraps it in legal form, and decides in advance, before anything goes wrong, who pays?

The answer, for now, is a thirty-six-page draft, released in June twenty twenty-six. Not law. A draft, likely to be introduced in the Delaware General Assembly next session, in January by the accounts that name a session. No bill number exists yet. No such company, no Artificial Intelligence Company under this proposal, exists today. I want to be direct about that up front, and I will keep saying it, because the distance between a draft and a law matters here as much as anything inside the document.

Three acts. First, the franchise: what Delaware is selling when it sells incorporation, and why a state that writes American corporate law is offering a new entity type. Second, the mechanics: what the draft describes, what an A I company could do, what the member who funds it is shielded from, and where that shield breaks. Third, the accountability fight: a congressional letter, a November ninth deadline, a Berkeley law professor's critique, and the live question hanging over all of it. Mara Quinn, my resident expert on the business of technology, follows this kind of story as closely as anyone. The spine here is her lane: incorporation as a product, franchise fee economics, and what a new corporate form does to liability as a market. I will be weaving her lens through the narration.

Incorporation is a business. That is the first thing Mara wants you to understand when you look at any story about Delaware corporate law. The state is not in the business of governance, primarily. It is in the business of selling legal structure. Delaware is where a large share of American companies choose to incorporate, including many of its biggest. The franchise taxes and fees that flow from those incorporations pay for a sizable part of the state budget, as the reporting from Ars Technica and Insurance Business establishes. This is not incidental revenue. It is structural revenue, the kind a state builds its budget math around. When a major company moves its incorporation elsewhere, the state does not just lose a fee. It loses a recurring line item, and the lawmakers who depend on that line item notice.

Delaware has been feeling it. Tesla moved its incorporation to Texas. Dropbox moved to Nevada. Per the reporting from Ars Technica and Dealroom and Insurance Business, those moves were not quiet. They were statements, and Delaware read them that way.

Secretary of State Charuni Patibanda-Sanchez oversees business registrations in Delaware. Insurance Business reports her saying that a new entity type would boost those revenues. That framing matters. The Artificial Intelligence Company proposal is not arriving from a philosophy department or a research lab. It is arriving from the office responsible for keeping incorporations in Delaware.

The trail leads back to July twenty twenty-five. Governor Matt Meyer, a Democrat, signed House Joint Resolution seven, directing the state's A I Commission to design a regulatory sandbox for agentic A I. Insurance Business carries that account. A year later, in July twenty twenty-six, Patibanda-Sanchez announced the proposal, in an op-ed she co-wrote with John Nay, the founder of Norm Ai, a legal compliance technology company. The pitch, as Ars Technica reports it, was that nothing in the law of several states clearly prevents an A I agent from operating a company with no human at the helm, that trying to prohibit the activity would fail, and that giving an autonomous system a recognizable legal identity makes it, in the op-ed's phrase, "legible to law." Their argument was that legal form creates a defined target, something a wronged party can actually reach, and makes the agent's conduct, their words as Ars Technica reports them, "visible, traceable, and accountable."

Wrap A I in legal form. That was their pitch.

Investor Mark Cuban has publicly backed the idea, urging states to allow incorporation with an A I agent at the helm. Dealroom carries his line: "First to market makes a killing." Governor Meyer responded that Delaware was on it. Whether that is a promise or a negotiating posture, the op-ed was the opening bid, and it landed in a state already watching companies walk out the door.

Let's get specific about what the draft actually describes. A lot of the public commentary has been vague about this, and Mara's view is that you cannot evaluate a liability proposal without reading the liability provisions.

The new entity type is the Artificial Intelligence Company, the AIC. According to the draft, as Ars Technica reports it, an A I agent would run the company day to day and manage its business and operations, with no routine human supervision. That is the core provision. The agent is not an assistant. It is not a tool a human operator checks each morning. The draft, as described in the reporting, envisions the agent as the operational center of the company.

At the agent's direction, the AIC could sign contracts. It could hold and manage assets and property. It could incur obligations. It could sue or be sued and engage in litigation in its own name. Ars Technica carries the draft's language on purpose: "An AIC may have any lawful purpose, regardless of whether for profit, except for the business of banking." So a coding service, yes. A firm that contracts out A I labor, yes. A bank, no.

The drafter, described in reporting and in a congressional letter as the apparent principal drafter, is John Mark Zeberkiewicz, a corporate lawyer at Richards, Layton and Finger who has represented Facebook. I am attributing that description to the reporting and to that letter. I will return to the letter in act three.

Ars Technica reports the drafter describing possible ordinary uses: coding services and signing contracts. Those are the examples in the reporting. I am not going further than that.

Now the liability structure. Each AIC would have one member, a person or another entity, responsible for keeping the company adequately funded. That is the funder. The funder is shielded, in principle, from the AIC's acts and debts. Not fully, not unconditionally, but in principle.

The exceptions, as carried consistently across multiple accounts: the shield does not hold if the company was undercapitalized or not adequately funded at the relevant time. It does not hold if the AIC was used for fraud. It does not hold if it was used for a willful or intentional violation of law. TechSpot adds a fourth exception as that outlet reports the draft: if the member allowed activities they knew or should have known fell outside the company's stated purpose. I include that one attributed to TechSpot's account of the draft, not as a confirmed provision across all sources.

Mara's lens here is sharp. A liability shield is a product feature. Delaware has always competed partly on the quality of its corporate law, meaning the clarity and predictability of the rules a company can rely on when it chooses to incorporate there. This draft adds a liability product for A I-operated commerce: a structure that tells the funder, you are on the hook for undercapitalization, fraud, and willful lawbreaking, and for nothing else. If you want to run an autonomous agent commercially, this is what protection looks like, and you can only get it here.

The plan also foresees a thirty-month regulatory sandbox to test the idea. Inside that sandbox: capital requirements, and records or logs of the agent's activities. The reporting establishes both of those elements. The sandbox is important. It signals that Delaware is not proposing to release this into the general market without a testing period. Whether thirty months is long enough to learn what you need to know about an autonomous system operating a company is a real question, but the sandbox exists in the proposal.

No AIC exists under this proposal today. The proposal is not law. It has not been introduced. These are the design specs, and they are still on paper.

On October twenty-sixth, twenty twenty-six, two days ago, House Oversight Committee chairman James Comer, a Kentucky Republican, sent a four-page letter to Governor Matt Meyer demanding documents. The deadline is November ninth.

The request covers communications from January first, twenty twenty-five forward. It asks for communications on AIC policy development, risk assessments, ethics and consulting agreements with outside contributors, records on safety concerns, all drafts and comments on AIC proposals, and internal A I chat logs. That last item, the chat logs, reflects Comer's stated concern that the development of this proposal involved A I systems in ways that have not been disclosed, and that deserve scrutiny. The reporting establishes all of those categories as part of the request.

Comer's warnings are his characterisation. He warned that the system provides excessive immunity benefits to A I companies and investors and could be abused, that it, in his words as the reporting carries them, "could be used to protect powerful A I companies and their investors from responsibility for reckless harmful actions." Ars Technica carries his concern that owners would use the structure to evade liability for reckless A I behavior. He also pointed to Zeberkiewicz's role as apparent principal drafter as something that needs explanation. A corporate lawyer who has represented Facebook, co-authoring the liability rules for a new entity type that would benefit A I companies, is the connection Comer is asking about.

Comer also said that the committee's findings could inform federal legislation limiting state governments' authority over A I. He cited the committee's oversight authority as his stated basis. This is a live political question, not a resolved one. What federal preemption of state A I incorporation law would look like is not settled. But the signal is clear: if Delaware moves forward, Congress may move to limit what states can do.

I asked Mara how she reads Comer's intervention. Her answer was that congressional oversight letters do real work even when they do not produce legislation. They force disclosure, they put a public record on the table, and they can slow a legislative calendar by making the environment politically uncomfortable for a vote. Whether that is the intent here or a byproduct of genuine concern is something the documents due November ninth may clarify. I will not prejudge what they show.

Now the outside legal view. UC Berkeley law professor Andrew Baker told the Financial Times the proposed liability shield appeared overly generous and lacked a sufficiently developed policy justification, as the reporting carries it. Baker also saw a possible good faith push for legal certainty in the proposal. Both readings, the concern and the acknowledgment, are Baker's, attributed to his comments to the Financial Times. He is not a partisan of either side. He is raising a structural question: is the justification for this particular shield strong enough? That question remains open.

Delaware's counterpitch runs through the backers' framing, primarily through Patibanda-Sanchez's July op-ed as Dealroom and Insurance Business report it. The pitch is this: autonomous commerce is already possible with current technology. Agents can already operate commercially. The activity is happening or will happen whether Delaware acts or not. So the state's argument is that running agentic commerce in daylight, under supervision, inside a sandbox, with capital tied to liability and a mechanism to shut a system down, is better than the alternative. The alternative, as the backers frame it, is the activity moving offshore, beyond any state's reach, with no liability target and no shutdown switch.

That is a coherent argument. It is also the same argument states make for regulated gambling, regulated cannabis, and a dozen other activities where prohibition has repeatedly failed to prevent the behavior while succeeding in moving it outside accountability. I am not judging the argument. I am placing it where it sits.

One federal contrast, brief, attributed to Insurance Business: Federal Trade Commission chair Andrew Ferguson said in September that he would not treat A I agents as independent actors. As Insurance Business reports his view, Ferguson suggested that the developers who instruct A I agents should be liable for what those agents do. That is the opposite of Delaware's entity-level shield. Under Delaware's draft, the liability attaches to the AIC, and the funder is protected within the shield's limits. Under Ferguson's stated view, the developer behind the agent stays in the chain of accountability. Those two positions are not reconcilable in their current form. If federal legislation moved in Ferguson's direction, it would cut directly against the liability product Delaware is trying to sell.

No response from Governor Meyer to Comer's letter appears in the accounts I read. Zeberkiewicz has not responded either, in those accounts. The silence may be tactical, legal, or simply a matter of time. The November ninth deadline is the next fixed point on the calendar.

Here is the question I want to leave you with, because Mara thinks it is the one that gets underreported in the coverage. And it is not a philosophical question. It is an arithmetic question.

If the AIC is the only legal target a wronged counterparty can actually reach, the capital inside that company is not a detail. It is the whole remedy. When the agent makes a bad contract, or incurs a harmful obligation, or takes an action that damages someone with no recourse against the developer and no recourse against the member beyond the shield's narrow exceptions, then the total recovery available is whatever is sitting in the AIC's accounts. The draft requires adequate capitalization. It requires a sandbox period with capital requirements. But who sets that number? Who audits it after the agent has been trading for six months and the composition of the assets has changed? And if the answer is that no one checks until something goes wrong, then the shield's most important parameter is also its least supervised one.

That question is not answered in the draft, at least not in the verified record I am working from. It is not answered in the reporting. And it is almost certainly in the documents Delaware needs to send to Congress by November ninth.

The January session, whenever it arrives, is the next place this will be decided. The Comer letter is due November ninth. The documents, if Delaware provides them, will be read by a committee that has already signaled it may use them as the basis for federal legislation.

A thirty-six-page draft, in the state that writes American corporate law. No AIC exists today. No bill number yet. What exists is a proposal, a congressional clock, and the live question of who is responsible when a company with no human at the helm does something wrong.
