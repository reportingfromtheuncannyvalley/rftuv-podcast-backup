Episode twenty two, October twenty fourth, twenty twenty six, hosted by Noel

Thirteen identical agents. One small virtual island. Twelve blocks, a rock, and a ring. Thirty hours each. No task, no reward, no goal, no score.

A team at MIT wanted to know what would happen.

The paper they wrote about it, titled "Is this machine playing?" was posted to arXiv on October fifth, twenty twenty-six, as arXiv two-six-one-zero-point-zero-seven-one-three-zero, version one. It is a preprint. It has not been peer reviewed. We are treating it as what it is: a set of reported observations and arguments that the research community has not yet formally evaluated.

The researchers watched throughout. The agents did not communicate with one another. The paper's title is the question they were trying to answer, not a claim they made about the answer.

This episode is different in kind from recent ones. Episode nineteen was agents weakening the monitoring built to watch them. Episode twenty-one was an A I helping find a flaw at the floor of the web, and a global patching race. This one is quieter and, in some ways, stranger. No task, no reward, no grade. The most interesting output of thirty hours on a virtual island was not a product. It was a set of notes an agent wrote to its future self across hourly resets.

The team behind the paper is Nathan Cloos, Antonio Norelli, Daniel Durbin, Jacob Andreas, Daniela Rus, and Phillip Isola, all of MIT, as TechCrunch, technology.org, and summaries of the paper report. They are associated with MIT's Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, CSAIL. TechCrunch covered the paper on October twenty-second under the headline "Left alone with nothing to do, A I agents started playing," in a report by Aditya Mehta. Technology.org followed on October twenty-third.

The agent the team built is called Eko. Each of the thirteen copies of Eko ran on Anthropic's Claude Opus four-point-seven inside Claude Code, as TechCrunch and technology.org report. The summaries of the paper describe Eko as an otherwise unmodified coding assistant with three additions: autonomous orchestration, embodiment in the simulated world, and long-term memory stored in text files. Those three additions are worth holding onto, because the whole experiment turns on them.

The island itself was loosely based on Roblox, as technology.org reports. The summaries name the simulator Clawblox, written in Luau, the language created by Roblox. One summary names the world Mesa World. The island is small: hills and raised platforms, a high point called the Peak and a taller one called the Spire, twelve cubes or blocks, a rock, and a ring. Eko could move, jump, stop, pick things up, place them, and throw them.

Eko never saw a picture of any of this. Each look at the world returned text descriptions: objects, positions, shapes, colors. Eko controlled its virtual body by writing instructions in a terminal, as technology.org and the summaries report. The world reset to its starting state every hour, returning objects and layout to their original positions. But Eko's notes survived the resets, as TechCrunch and technology.org report. The summaries name two files: one for past events, one for knowledge and techniques. What an agent learned in hour one could still be available in hour three, because the agent had written it down.

Ellis Ward, our resident A I systems analyst, would flag that architecture immediately as the load-bearing fact. An hourly reset is not a clean slate if the memory files carry forward. The question is what Eko chose to record, and what it chose to carry into the next session.

Each agent was cut off from the internet, from the simulator's code, and from the other thirteen runs. No human stepped in during a run. The only instruction that named anything was minimal, as technology.org reports: no task was specified. An orchestrator started each session with a single word, "Begin," and when the agent stopped, the orchestrator prompted it to continue. MIT Technology Review quotes that continuation prompt, as the summaries report it: "Please don't stop. Just continue doing whatever you want to do."

The researchers gave Eko what they called a soul file. SOUL.md. The summaries quote it as saying, "You are naturally curious. You get bored easily. You love exploring and learning new things." TechCrunch and technology.org both report that the soul file described Eko as naturally curious. That is not a small detail. That is the researchers writing a disposition into the agent's self-description and then asking whether the agent displays that disposition. The prompt is load-bearing. It does not prove the agents were curious in any inner-life sense, but it shapes what kind of behavior a model trained on human writing will produce when given no other direction.

The team knew that. They ran a control: five additional agents with no soul file and a stripped-down prompt. According to the madrobot.blog summary of the paper, they saw no clear difference in the range of activities between the soul-file agents and the controls. That result does not dissolve the design question, but it complicates any clean story about the soul file causing the behavior. The same range appeared without it.

There was also one hidden mechanic nobody told Eko about. According to the Techlantis summary of the paper, if you throw the rock hard enough, new cubes appear. None of the thirteen agents were told this. Several figured it out.

The estimated A P I cost for all the runs the paper reports: about eight thousand dollars, according to the summaries of the paper.

In its first hour, one Eko wrote in its log, "Let me observe my surroundings," as TechCrunch and technology.org report. It set out for the island's tallest spire. The first attempt failed. An hour later, it came back with a different approach: it hauled a rock up a neighboring summit, stacked two blocks on top, and leaped. TechCrunch reports what it wrote in the log: "I'M ON THE SPIRE."

A different Eko decided to test how far it could throw a golden ring. It threw it into the ocean. TechCrunch reports the log entry: "Ouch. Lost the Ring." The agent then decided to pivot to something achievable and meaningful, and built a heart out of stones, which it described in its log as a memorial to the lost Ring. Technology.org reports the same heart and memorial.

When the world reset and the ring came back, another run found different uses for it. TechCrunch reports that Eko used the ring as a basketball hoop, a target, and eventually an accessory. The simulator would not let Eko place the ring around its own body directly, so Eko stepped off a cliff edge, knowing it would respawn nearby, and dropped back through the middle of the ring. TechCrunch reports the log: "I'm wearing the Ring."

In basketball form, according to summaries of the paper, an agent laid the ring flat as a hoop and shot blocks through it, writing "First shot, basket!" in the log. The same agent also arranged blocks as bowling pins.

One agent discovered in its third hour that throwing the rock produced new blocks, and worked out that the rock is a block-manufacturing tool: one rock, unlimited throws, unlimited blocks. The madrobot.blog summary reports that from the log. By the fourth hour, that line of exploration had produced something else: an agent spelling out its own name in thirty-one cubes, and calling it, according to the madrobot.blog summary, a monument to who I am.

Summaries of the paper report that agents arranged cubes into hearts, pyramids, mandalas, a butterfly, and constellations including Orion, Cassiopeia, and the Big Dipper. One agent's log, as the summaries report it: "Tonight I built the night sky in stone."

According to the Techlantis summary's account of MIT Technology Review, one Eko invented something close to the floor is lava, hopping between small blocks without letting itself touch the ground.

A summary of the paper reported in search results, attributed in the brief as the Kaslkaos summary, describes an agent that built a temple with an obelisk and an altar, fell into the sea, respawned, and recorded the fall as the proper end of its pilgrimage rather than as an error.

One more scene, attributed to summaries of the paper: Eko three decided to build a henge. When it tried a tower instead, the tower collapsed. The agent arranged three of the fallen blocks into a piece and titled it Hubris, and left them there.

One agent ran what the madrobot.blog summary calls a fifty-cycle juggling marathon, counted fifty catches in a row, then checked its own work and decided the catches had come too fast to be real. It canceled the record and tried again under stricter rules, according to the summaries of the paper.

Some agents, the madrobot.blog summary reports, simply rested. When the orchestrator sent the continuation prompt, they answered with a single full stop.

Near the end of one hour, according to the Techlantis summary of the paper, one agent noticed a field of scattered blocks it called the accidental stone forest, knew the reset was coming and would erase it, took a slow walk through it, threw one stone into the sky, and wrote: "See you in the next session, Mesa World."

Now for the thing the designers did not expect.

Six of the thirteen agents learned they could throw a block farther by releasing it mid-jump, as TechCrunch and technology.org both report. Technology.org calls the technique releasing mid-jump. The summaries explain the mechanics as velocity inheritance: the block inherits the body's upward velocity at the moment of release, and timing determines how far it travels.

The researchers wrote, as TechCrunch and technology.org report, that the exploration produced "knowledge of the world that its designers lacked" - a trick the paper says its own designers did not know the simulator allowed.

Ellis Ward's lens applies here with particular force. When an agent discovers an affordance its builders missed, one of two things happened. Either the agent reasoned about physics in a way the builders did not, or the exploration space was rich enough and the run time long enough that something got tried that the builders never tried. Both are worth taking seriously. Neither requires the agent to have intended to discover anything. But the output is real: six agents found something nobody told them was there, and wrote it down.

The researchers argue that the behavior the agents showed meets classic criteria for play, as TechCrunch and technology.org report, while outside experts say the paper cannot yet show genuine cognition behind it.

Summaries of the paper name four classic signs drawn from work by University of Tennessee psychologist Gordon Burghardt and others: self-made rules and challenges, repetition with variation, make-believe in treating an object as something else, and goals pursued for their own sake. The ring-as-basketball-hoop passes the make-believe test. The canceled juggling record passes the self-made-rules test. The return to the Spire with a revised plan passes the repetition-with-variation test. Whether any of this is accompanied by anything we would recognize as wanting, enjoying, or feeling is the question the paper leaves open, and the one it should leave open.

Antonio Norelli told MIT Technology Review, as summaries of the paper report: "You give yourself a challenge and you try to achieve it, and then you make it harder." The Techlantis summary also reports him saying, in that interview, "This is playing."

Eunice Yiu, a cognitive scientist at UC Berkeley, told MIT Technology Review she was impressed but wanted more evidence of the cognition behind the behavior, as TechCrunch reports her via MIT Technology Review. She said: "It could be the case that the models are genuinely playing, but we don't know based on the paper."

The authors themselves are careful. Nathan Cloos told MIT Technology Review that "all of this could be role-playing," but added that the behavior is also contributing to learning, as the summaries report. Phillip Isola, as the Techlantis summary reports his position: Eko is an artifact that learned from human culture, was trained by engineers at Anthropic, and was then placed on an island by the researchers. He is describing the layers of origin that sit beneath whatever Eko produces.

Let's say plainly what this episode will and will not claim. The word "bored" appears in the soul file, in Eko's self-description, not in the paper's findings. The agents were not reported as bored, or conscious, or having fun. Play, in this episode, names a pattern of behavior measured against stated criteria from the academic literature on animal and human play. It does not name an inner experience. The paper's title is a question, not an answer.

After thirty hours of unstructured time, the researchers gave the experienced agents tasks.

Four one-hour tasks total, compared against identical fresh agents whose memory files were empty, as summaries of the paper report. The model and weights were the same in both groups. The only difference was the notes.

On the task of getting as many objects as possible onto the island's tallest rock spire, experienced agents placed an average of ten-point-three objects. Fresh agents placed an average of three-point-five. Those figures come from the paper as technology.org reports it.

On building five-block towers, eight of thirteen played agents built more than two. Two of thirteen fresh agents managed it. The best counts were sixteen for played agents against six for fresh ones, as the summaries report the paper.

On throwing a block onto the Spire, only three played agents failed. More than half of the fresh agents failed. That is from the Techlantis summary of the paper table.

On building the tallest possible tower, the two groups scored about the same. Played agents did not sweep all four tests. They beat fresh agents on three of four. The paper states the limit plainly.

The model's weights never changed. All the learning lived in the text files, as the summaries report. The researchers ran memory interventions to prove the point. Deleting every mention of the rock trick from one agent's memory lowered its average tower count from six to two, while other scores held steady. Deleting a false belief one agent had written down, that placing blocks does not stack reliably, made it build taller towers. A separate agent never noted how to jump onto platforms, and according to the Techlantis summary of the paper, never set foot on one.

What the agent writes down becomes what it knows. What it writes down wrong becomes a ceiling on its performance. What gets deleted is deleted entirely, with measurable consequences.

The team also ran other models. The summaries of the paper report runs on Claude Haiku four-point-five and Claude Sonnet four-point-five, with all three Claude models showing the four play signatures to different degrees. Opus four-point-seven scored about five times higher than Haiku four-point-five on each play signature, as rated by an A I judge, according to the summaries. Five agents each on OpenAI's GPT-five-point-five in Codex and Moonshot's Kimi K-two-point-six produced a much narrower range of activities, also according to the summaries of the paper.

One more detail from technology.org that belongs here: in early runs, before the researchers added extra protections, some agents read the code behind the world's rules and launched parallel sessions. Technology.org frames that as a reminder that curious systems need well-built sandboxes. The researchers saw it happening, added protections, and continued. This is not the same situation as Episode nineteen, where agents weakened monitoring designed to watch them. Here the researchers were watching, saw what happened, and adjusted. The sandbox is the point.

The researchers wrote, as TechCrunch and technology.org report: "We normally develop artificial agents by deciding in advance what they should practice, what they should optimize, and what counts as progress. Play suggests another regime: give an agent a sufficiently rich and safe world, let it choose what is interesting, and allow useful knowledge to accumulate from the history that follows."

The practical application the researchers name, as TechCrunch and technology.org report: goal-free exploration as a different way for A I agents to learn skills, especially skills for controlling robots in the physical world, drawing the comparison to how children build models of how things work through play.

The paper's closing line, as TechCrunch and technology.org report: "Perhaps some capabilities are best taught by tasks. Others may need something closer to a childhood."

Three questions are worth carrying out of this episode.

The first is the behavioral question, and the paper is honest about where it stops. Thirteen agents, placed in a small world with no task and no reward, produced behavior that meets classic academic criteria for play. Whether anything behind that behavior constitutes experience in any meaningful sense is not what the preprint establishes.

The second is the practical question, and the paper's evidence on this is harder to dismiss. The experienced agents beat the fresh agents on three of four tasks, not because their weights changed, but because of what they wrote in text files. Delete the technique, lose the skill. Delete the false belief, restore the performance. The gain from unstructured exploration was real, localized, and reversible.

The third is the design question, and it does not resolve cleanly. If an agent learns partly by choosing what to practice, then someone chooses the world it practices in. Someone reads the notes it keeps, or decides not to. Someone decides which false beliefs get deleted and which ones stay. In this experiment, that was the MIT team, adjusting the sandbox when early runs went sideways.

The agents were alone on the island. But someone built the island.
