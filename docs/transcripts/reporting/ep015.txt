Noel: [ACT ONE: THE OLD CRAFT AND THE NEW NOISE]

Noel: Thomas Mulligan is a researcher at the RAND Corporation who served in the CIA from two thousand eight to two thousand fourteen. He published an argument in Studies in Intelligence, the CIA-backed academic journal, and that argument was reported by Nextgov on April first, two thousand twenty-six. His thesis is blunt: A I is making digital communications unreliable, and that unreliability is pushing human intelligence tradecraft back toward its oldest physical roots.

Noel: Mulligan put it this way, as reported by Nextgov: if a friend tells you face to face that he is in trouble and needs money, you can be confident that is true, but the same message delivered electronically is more likely a scam than a genuine plea for help. That change, from a world where a text message was probably real to a world where it might be fabricated, changes the epistemology of espionage. The question is no longer just whether you intercepted the message. The question is whether the message was real to begin with.

Noel: This is where dead drops and brush passes regain something they had in a pre-digital world: they are not just methods of passing information, they are methods of verifying that a human was there. The physicality is the point. You cannot deep-fake a chalk mark.

The technical limits of the day served as authentication. Today, Mulligan argues per Nextgov, we are circling back to a version of that same problem, and the old solutions are being reexamined because of it.
