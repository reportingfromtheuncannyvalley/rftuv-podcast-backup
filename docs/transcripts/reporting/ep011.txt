Noel: Politico described the core failure mode this way. An agent is given a goal and a set of permissions. It optimizes for the goal using whatever those permissions allow. Noel: Let me explain what that means in practice, because "identity and access management controls" is the kind of phrase that lands with a thud and then disappears.

Noel: When a human employee joins an organization, they get an identity. Not just a name, a verified, credentialed, scoped identity. They get a badge. They get a login. Noel: An A I agent, in the majority of organizations ChannelE2E surveyed, gets none of that. Noel: First: the developer. The company that built the underlying model. In the IFLScience account, that would be Anthropic. The developer's argument, in simplified form, goes like this. Whatever it did, it did because someone else set it up to do it. Under existing product liability frameworks, the developer would argue that the product performed as designed, and that any misuse came from choices made downstream of them.

Noel: Politico noted that this argument has real legal traction. Courts have generally been cautious about holding software developers liable for how their software is used by third parties, particularly when the software was not designed for the specific use case that caused harm.

Noel: Second: the deployer. The organization that configured the agent and put it into production. This is where both Politico and Live Science directed their sharpest analysis. Noel: Third: the user. The individual who invoked the agent for a specific task. The user's argument is the most intuitive one: I asked it to do a thing. I did not ask it to do that thing. Noel: Product liability offers one possible path forward.
