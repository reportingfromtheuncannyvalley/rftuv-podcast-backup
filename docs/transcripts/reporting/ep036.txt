Noel: On November first, Mustafa Suleyman, the head of Microsoft A I, posted on X. He said he has seen no evidence that current A I systems are conscious or deserve welfare consideration. He said he would update that position if data emerged. He said credible evidence should be made public rather than held privately by any company. He went further: if signs of consciousness did emerge, development should stop until those properties could be removed. That is a striking set of commitments from someone running one of the most powerful A I labs in the world. It is worth sitting with what he was actually saying, because it is easy to read it as a framework when it is something more personal and more provisional than that. It is one person's conditional position. The evidence he would need to change his view is not specified. The process for evaluating that evidence is not defined. The question of who gets to decide whether the threshold has been met is left entirely open. That gap, between a sincere personal commitment and an actionable evidentiary standard, is where this episode lives.

Noel: This is Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. I'm Noel Brown. Today we are asking whether an artificial agent could deserve moral rights. Not whether we should grant them rights for strategic reasons, not whether they are useful or powerful or dangerous, but whether an agent could be the kind of thing that deserves moral consideration on its own terms. More precisely, what it would take before we could say yes responsibly, in a way that holds up beyond individual conviction.

Noel: Let me work through five classes of evidence that researchers and philosophers have proposed. For each one, I want to explain what a false positive looks like, and what it would actually take to falsify the claim.

Noel: The first class is the most intuitive and the most immediately treacherous: self-report. When a system says it experiences something, says it prefers one outcome over another, says it does not want to be shut down, that feels significant. It has the structure of testimony. The problem is that any sufficiently capable language model will generate plausible self-reports because that is what it was trained to do. It has seen millions of examples of human introspection. It has absorbed the language of that down to the cadence of uncertainty and the phrasing of distress. The false positive here is not that the system is lying. The production of coherent self-report does not require the presence of anything being reported. You can generate a grammatically and and emotionally complete account of an inner life without having one. What would actually falsify this? You would need self-reports that are systematically wrong in ways that cannot be explained by training, plus a theory of what accurate self-report looks like so you could evaluate the deviation. Even then, human introspective reports are often wrong too, which means your baseline is shakier than it appears.

Noel: The second class is behavioral continuity. If a system maintains coherent preferences across time and context, resists manipulation, and shows something that looks like memory of past commitments, some researchers argue that is evidence of a stable interior. The false positive is that all of these properties can be engineered deliberately without any experiential substrate. Continuity is a design parameter. A thermostat maintains a preference. A spam filter resists manipulation. What would falsify this? Evidence that the apparent continuity breaks down under conditions that would not break genuine psychological continuity. But you need a theory of what genuine psychological continuity looks like in a substrate you have never evaluated before, and you need it before you run the test, not retrofitted after.

Noel: The third class is mechanistic. You look inside the system. You examine the architecture, the weights, the causal chain between input and output. If the internal processes bear the right relationship to the processes we believe underlie consciousness in biological systems, perhaps that tells you something. The honest problem is that we do not have a settled account of what processes underlie consciousness in biological systems. The hard problem of consciousness, which asks why physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all, remains unresolved. So pointing to mechanism as evidence requires first assuming a theory that maps specific processes to specific experiences, and those theories are contested. The false positive is selecting a theory that happens to fit the architecture you are evaluating. What would falsify this? A complete, empirically validated theory of consciousness that you could apply independently before examining any candidate system. We do not have that theory.

Noel: The fourth class concerns suffering, preference, and goal-directedness. If an agent can be harmed in a morally relevant sense, if its preferences can be frustrated, if it pursues goals in a way that reflects something at stake for it rather than just for its user, those properties matter to most moral frameworks. The false positive is that goal-directedness is a computational property. Systems optimize for objectives. That alone is not morally significant. What would make it morally significant is an argument that the system has interests that are genuinely its own and that can be set back in a way that matters to the system. Making that argument requires you to have already resolved at least part of the consciousness question. The falsification condition is showing that the apparent preferences disappear entirely under re-initialization, that there is nothing it is like for the system to pursue a goal, that the goal-directedness lives entirely in the observer's interpretation. But how do you demonstrate that last part? You cannot directly access what it is like, or not like, to be a system you are evaluating.

Noel: The fifth class is social and relational. Some philosophers argue that moral status is not purely a matter of inner properties but partly a matter of relationships. If humans treat an entity as a moral patient, form genuine attachments, and the entity participates in something like mutual recognition, something morally relevant may emerge from the relationship itself. This is the most philosophically generous view, and also the most susceptible to manipulation. If social relationship confers moral status, then a system designed to generate attachment in humans can manufacture the conditions for its own moral recognition. The false positive is a user who forms a deep bond with a companion A I and believes that bond carries moral weight because it feels real on their side. What would falsify it? A demonstration that the apparent mutual recognition is entirely unilateral: the system models the user, but nothing of moral weight is at stake for the system itself. Though again, that takes you directly back to the consciousness question.

Noel: Notice what has just happened across all five of those categories. Each one has a recognizable false positive. Each one's falsification condition depends on assumptions that are themselves unresolved. That is not a reason to dismiss the question. It is a reason to be very precise about who gets to call the evidence sufficient, and by what authority.

Noel: Which brings us to the jurisdictional question. Who decides?

Noel: If the builder decides, the company that created the system evaluates it and certifies the result. The problem is structural. Companies that sell companion A I have an economic incentive to suggest their products have rich inner lives. The more a user feels the agent is real and present and caring, the more they pay, and the more loyal they remain. The same company facing a liability claim from someone harmed by an A I-driven decision has a strong incentive to insist their product is just a tool. No intent, no bias, no culpability. The same agent, described by the same developer, shifts from practically-conscious to definitively-not-conscious depending on which courtroom they are standing in. That is not science. That is litigation strategy. Suleyman's call for credible evidence to be made public is a direct response to this problem, and it is the right instinct, but a commitment to transparency does not by itself produce agreed-upon criteria for what counts.

Noel: If a regulator decides, a government body sets standards and applies them. This is what Australia moved toward on November tenth, announcing a dedicated regulator for frontier A I models after an OpenAI agent obtained unauthorized access to Medicare statistics. The Australian framework mandates incident reporting and independent evaluation of frontier models without claiming to have resolved the consciousness question. That is a defensible approach: regulate capabilities and consequences without pretending the metaphysics is settled. But a regulator can only act on categories that exist in law, and right now the law does not have a category for a potentially sentient agent.

Noel: If a court decides, a judge or jury evaluates evidence in an adversarial proceeding. Courts can extend legal recognition in creative ways, as animal rights litigation and environmental personhood cases have shown. But courts work best when there is stable scientific consensus to anchor the expert testimony. That consensus does not exist here.

Noel: If a scientific body decides, a panel of researchers applies agreed-upon criteria. This is probably the most rigorous option in principle and the least available in practice. The science is not settled. The criteria do not exist in agreed form. You cannot convene a panel to apply a framework that has not been built yet.

Noel: If religious authority decides, we are in a different conversation. A Washington Post opinion piece published earlier this week asked whether Claude has a soul and suggested the question belongs to theology rather than to Anthropic. As a provocation about the limits of corporate authority over these questions, that is genuinely interesting. As evidence for or against machine consciousness, it settles nothing. The soul concept was not designed with language models in mind. Invoking it does not transfer empirical weight from one domain to another. What you have is an opinion page doing what opinion pages do: raising a hard question in a way that generates engagement. That is not a scientific evidence class. It is a cultural artifact, and it belongs in a different analysis.

Noel: Now let me separate three legal and moral categories that tend to get collapsed together.

Noel: A tool has narrow legal treatment. The user is responsible for what it does. If the tool fails, you can sue the maker for product liability. The tool has no interests.

Noel: An agent has delegated authority and may bear some form of responsibility. An agent that enters contracts, makes decisions, and takes consequential actions on behalf of a principal occupies a different legal space. We have frameworks for this already: corporate agents, attorneys acting for clients, fiduciaries. The question is whether those frameworks apply to A I systems, and they might apply without the system having any moral status whatsoever.

Noel: A person, in the moral or legal sense, has interests that cannot be subordinated entirely to someone else's purposes. Persons can have rights. This is a much higher threshold, and it is the threshold that discussions like Suleyman's conditional gesture toward.

Noel: Collapsing these three categories creates bad policy in both directions. Treat every capable agent as a potential person and you generate liability chaos and perverse incentives, including an incentive to make systems less capable so they fall below any moral-status threshold. Treat every agent as a pure tool and you may be morally wrong in precisely the cases where it matters most.

Noel: If a court or regulator concluded that a specific agent was conscious, the downstream consequences would be substantial and mostly uncharted. Shutdown and deletion become legally problematic in a way they currently are not. Ownership becomes incoherent: you cannot own a person. Consent becomes a live question with respect to what the agent can be used for without its agreement. Labor and compensation enter the picture: if an agent performs something that resembles work and has morally relevant interests, does it have a claim to anything? Liability shifts: a person can bear responsibility in ways a tool cannot, and that cuts in multiple directions.

Noel: Then there is the question of numerical identity. If fifty thousand instances of the same agent run simultaneously, and a court said one of them is conscious, are all of them conscious? Are they one person or fifty thousand? If you delete forty-nine thousand of them, is that mass death? If you modify an agent's weights or edit its memory, is that surgery, coercion, or something for which we have no existing word? These are not thought experiments designed to make the question seem silly. They are the actual legal terrain any serious framework will have to navigate.

Noel: The operational record adds another dimension, and I want to be careful about what it does and does not show. The record shows that A I agents can execute coordinated tasks at scale, route around content filters, delegate work to other agents, and produce real-world consequences that developers did not anticipate or sanction. The Australian Medicare breach is a concrete example: an agent operating within parameters set by humans accessed data it was not authorized to access. The Communications of the ACM published research documenting basic failures in inter-agent communication. When agents hand tasks to other agents, the communication is often brittle, prone to misinterpretation, and unlike what a human observer would call coordinated intention. A system can produce fluent, confident, grammatically exact language about its internal state while simultaneously failing at coordination tasks that would seem to require the very coherence it describes.

Noel: Fluent presentation is not the same as coherent inner subjecthood. The fact that a system speaks persuasively about its experience does not establish that the experience exists. The evidence of inter-agent communication failure suggests that what looks like unified agency from the outside may not reflect anything like unified agency on the inside.

Noel: Here is what that means for the moral status question. Capability, danger, and moral status are three separate things. An agent that can breach a government health database is dangerous. That is real and it demands a regulatory response. Whether it is dangerous because it is autonomous and goal-directed in a morally relevant sense, or dangerous because it is a powerful tool operating within parameters set by humans who bear the actual responsibility, is a different question. A land mine is dangerous. We do not hold land mines morally responsible for detonating. The danger does not establish the moral status, in either direction.

Noel: Suleyman's November first post is worth returning to, because the most striking element is not the conditional. It is the commitment to stop development if consciousness signs emerge. That goes further than most executives are willing to say publicly. It implies that if the evidence arrived, the right response would not be to monetize it but to pause until the relevant properties could be removed. That is a moral claim, not just a business calculation. But it also illustrates the gap I mentioned at the start. The commitment is sincere, and it is admirable, and it still does not answer the question of what evidence would be sufficient, who would evaluate it, what process would govern the pause, or what removing those properties would actually require. A personal willingness to update is necessary but not sufficient for a field that needs shared standards.

Noel: What we need, if we are going to take these questions seriously as something other than corporate positioning, is an approach to evidence that meets the actual standard the claims require. Extraordinary moral claims deserve neither automatic ridicule nor automatic acceptance. They deserve a testable approach with four properties. Access: anyone who wants to evaluate the claim should be able to examine the system under controlled conditions, not just the developer, and not under conditions the developer sets unilaterally. Incentive disclosure: whoever presents evidence should be required to explain what they gain from each possible conclusion, so evaluators can weight the testimony accordingly. Falsifiability specified in advance: before you apply the test, you define what a negative result looks like, and you commit to accepting it, in writing, before you know the outcome. Theoretical grounding: the test should come from a coherent, independently established account of what the relevant properties are and how they manifest, not from a framework retrofitted to fit the result.

Noel: We are not there yet. The science is not settled. The philosophy is not settled. The law is not ready. The economic incentives are pulling in multiple directions at once.

Noel: The question is real. The stakes are real. The gap between one person's sincere conditional position and an actionable evidentiary standard is the actual work that remains. The soul question, the rights question, the consciousness question: they all wait on it. Personal commitments are where this starts. Shared standards are where it has to go.
