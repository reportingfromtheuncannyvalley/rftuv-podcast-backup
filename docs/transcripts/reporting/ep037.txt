Noel: On Monday, November sixteenth, a governor of the Federal Reserve stood up at a finance conference in Milan and described a bank run that has never happened. Not a run that did happen and got studied afterward. A run that could happen, at a speed no bank run in history has ever moved, because the depositors doing the running would not be people. They would be software, acting for people, all at once, in minutes.

Noel: The governor is Michael Barr. The speech was called Some Potential Financial Stability Implications of Artificial Intelligence, delivered at the seventh annual International Conference on Artificial Intelligence in Finance, at Bocconi University. And the sentence at the center of it deserves to be heard slowly. Barr said that where customers once needed to execute transfers themselves, A I agents could instantaneously detect yield differentials or stability signals and autonomously move funds at high speed, potentially accelerating runs that unfold in minutes, overwhelming traditional liquidity management and regulatory intervention timelines.

Noel: This is Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. I am Noel. Today, one story, taken slowly. The Fed is worried that A I agents could trigger a bank run. We will work through what Barr actually said, and what he did not say. We will explain the mechanism, because the mechanism is the story. We will compare it honestly with the fastest real bank run on record, which happened in twenty twenty three. And we will look at what supervisors and banks can actually do about it, including a new examiner framework from the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency that landed less than two weeks before Barr spoke.

Noel: Our resident expert on the business mechanics here is Mara Quinn, who covers the business of technology for us and wrote about this warning for R F T U V today. Her framing is the one we will keep returning to. This episode is not really about whether an agent can move money. Agents can already move money. It is about what happens to a banking system built on human hesitation when the hesitation is engineered out.

Noel: First, what Barr actually said, because precision matters here and this story is already being told looser than he told it.

Noel: Barr was careful to say he was speaking for himself, not necessarily for his colleagues on the Federal Reserve Board. That is standard language for a Fed governor, but it matters. This was a warning from one senior regulator thinking out loud in public, not a rule, not a proposal, and not a finding that any bank is in danger today. Bloomberg reported the speech on Monday under the headline that Barr says A I agents could fuel future bank runs. Future is the operative word in that headline, and we are going to keep it there.

Noel: He made two distinct points, and they are worth separating, because the coverage tends to blur them into one.

Noel: The first point is about permission, and it is not hypothetical at all. Barr said major banks now report finding A I agents attempting to take actions in retail customer accounts, without the bank being able to verify the authenticity of the agent, the consent of the customer, or whether what the agent was doing actually matched what the customer intended. Sit with that for a moment. This is not a stress scenario. This is banks, today, seeing software show up at the door of ordinary customer accounts and try to act, and the bank cannot reliably tell whether a human being sent it, agreed to it, or even knows about it. The agent might be a helpful tool the customer switched on and forgot. It might be something else. The bank, at the moment of the attempted action, cannot always tell the difference.

Noel: The second point is the speed point, the bank run point, and that one is a scenario, clearly labelled as one by Barr himself. If agents can watch interest rates and watch for signs of trouble at a bank, and if they are authorized to move deposits when they see those signals, then in a moment of stress many agents could move many deposits at the same time, far faster than their human owners could have done it one by one. Runs that once unfolded over days could unfold in minutes. And the tools regulators and banks use to stop runs, the statements, the phone calls, the emergency facilities, the careful public reassurance, all of those tools were built for human timescales. They assume there is time. Barr is asking what happens when there is not.

Noel: He also named what he thinks the guardrails should be, and again, he was specific. Strict authentication, so a bank knows what is acting. Explicit consent verification for specific actions, so permission is tied to the actual transaction and not granted once, vaguely, forever. Technical controls that limit what an agent is capable of doing in the first place. And both rules based and values based constraints on agent behavior. Four guardrails. None of them is a ban. Barr is not arguing against agents in finance. He is arguing that the permission layer has to be built before the speed layer scales, and his speech suggests he thinks the order is currently backwards.

Noel: There is a backdrop to the timing. On Friday, November thirteenth, the Federal Reserve published its latest Financial Stability Report, and in the survey behind it, eighty seven percent of the market contacts the Fed polls named artificial intelligence as a salient risk to the financial system, up from fifty percent in the spring. A I topped the list. Barr's speech, three days later, reads like the detailed version of that number.

Noel: Now, the mechanism. Why does speed change a bank run, rather than just making the same run happen a bit sooner?

Noel: A bank run is, at its core, a coordination problem wearing a panic costume. A bank holds only a fraction of its deposits as cash ready to go, because the rest is lent out or invested. That is not fraud, it is the design of banking, and it works perfectly well as long as withdrawals arrive at a normal, staggered pace. A run is what happens when a large number of depositors decide at the same time that being last in line is the one unacceptable outcome. Each individual decision can be perfectly rational. The collective result can kill a healthy looking bank in an afternoon, because the bank cannot turn loans into cash at the speed of fear.

Noel: What historically slowed runs down was friction, and agents remove almost every item on that list. A worried depositor had to hear the news, believe it, decide to act, and then execute a transfer. Each step took time, and time did work. During those hours, other depositors pushed back with calmer readings. The bank issued statements. Regulators made calls. Some people slept on it and woke up less afraid. Human depositors also disagree with each other. They read different sources and have different thresholds. That disagreement is not noise. It is a shock absorber. A crowd that panics at different speeds is much easier to save than a crowd that panics in unison.

Noel: Now replace the crowd with agents. An agent does not hear news the way you do. It monitors feeds continuously. It does not weigh whether a rumor feels credible over coffee. It evaluates signals against rules it was given, and if the conditions are met, it acts. It does not sleep on it. It does not feel embarrassment about being wrong in public, because it is not in public. And here is the part Mara Quinn's reporting for us today put most sharply. Thousands of agents, built by different companies for different customers, may still be watching the same news sources, the same market data, and the same social signals, and applying broadly similar logic to them. When the signal fires, they do not panic at different speeds. They execute at the same speed, in the same direction, at roughly the same moment. The shock absorber is gone. What replaces it is correlation, and correlation is the thing that turns a worry into a wave.

Noel: There is a second, quieter mechanism in Barr's speech that does not require panic at all. He mentioned yield differentials in the same breath as stability signals. An agent does not need to be afraid to move your money. It only needs to notice that another bank is paying more. Chasing yield once took effort, so most deposits were sticky. An agent has no inertia. If moving money for a better rate is inside its instructions, it will do it continuously, at scale. That makes every bank's deposit base less stable in ordinary times, thinner ice under the whole system, so when a genuine stability signal arrives, less friction holds deposits in place.

Noel: So that is the mechanism. Speed, correlation, and the removal of friction, applied to the oldest fragility in banking. Now the honest comparison, because we have a recent, real, measured case of how fast a modern run can move with no agents involved at all, and any serious version of this story has to reckon with it.

Noel: In March of twenty twenty three, Silicon Valley Bank failed. The facts, briefly. The bank had grown very fast and had placed a large share of its deposits into long term government backed bonds, which had fallen in value as interest rates rose. About ninety four percent of its deposits were uninsured, above the two hundred fifty thousand dollar federal insurance limit, because its customers were mostly technology companies holding operating cash, not households holding savings. On March eighth, the bank announced it had sold about twenty one billion dollars of securities at a loss of about one point eight billion dollars, and planned to raise about two point two five billion dollars in new capital. The announcement was meant to reassure. It did the opposite. On March ninth, in a single day, customers withdrew forty two billion dollars. That is roughly a quarter of the bank's deposits, gone in about ten hours, moved by app and by wire while venture capital firms advised their portfolio companies, in group chats and on social media, to get out. The bank told supervisors it expected more than one hundred billion dollars more to leave the next day. Regulators closed it on March tenth instead.

Noel: Forty two billion dollars in a day, with human beings making every decision. That is the benchmark. So what, honestly, is different about the agent scenario Barr is describing? Three things, and only three, but they compound.

Noel: First, the decision step disappears. At Silicon Valley Bank, every withdrawal still passed through a person who had to be reached, persuaded, and moved to act during business hours. An agent removes the reaching and the persuading. The instruction was given in advance, in calm times, and it executes in stressed times without asking again. The run no longer needs a persuasive moment. It needs only a triggering signal.

Noel: Second, the population changes. The twenty twenty three run was driven by a concentrated, networked, mostly uninsured depositor base with enormous balances and every reason to move first. A retail agent run could involve vastly more accounts, each much smaller, many of them insured, acting through software rather than through fear. Insurance matters here, and honesty requires saying so. An insured depositor has far less reason to run, because the guarantee does not depend on being first in line, so some of the classic run logic weakens. But agents optimizing yield, or following a stability rule their owner barely remembers setting, may move money for reasons unrelated to the owner's actual risk. The guarantee protects the depositor. It does not protect the bank from the outflow.

Noel: Third, the response window collapses. In twenty twenty three, regulators had a weekend. The bank was closed on a Friday, and by Sunday night the authorities had assembled a response that protected all depositors and calmed the wider system. That weekend was not a luxury. It was the workspace in which the rescue was built. A run that unfolds in minutes, Barr's phrase, does not offer a weekend. It barely offers a meeting. The honest conclusion is not that agents invent a new kind of crisis. It is that they take the fastest crisis banking already has and remove the two things that made it survivable, the pause while humans decide, and the pause while officials respond.

Noel: Which brings us to the last part of the episode. What can actually be done, and what is already on paper?

Noel: Start with Barr's four guardrails, because they map directly onto the failure modes. Authentication answers the uninvited agent at the account door. Per action consent answers the vague, one time permission that an agent stretches across a thousand later decisions. Technical limits answer the agent that was authorized to do something small and attempts something large. And rules based and values based constraints are an attempt to give agents something human tellers and human customers have always supplied informally, a sense of what not to do even when the instructions, read literally, allow it.

Noel: Then there is the supervisory layer, and this is where the OCC framework comes in. On November fourth, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency issued Bulletin twenty twenty six fifty four, a supervisory framework for how its examiners should evaluate banks' use of what the government now calls super intelligence, including generative and agentic systems. Three facts about that framework matter for this story, and they cut in different directions.

Noel: The first is what it is not. It is an examiner tool, not a rule. The OCC states plainly that it does not create new enforceable standards or new supervisory expectations for banks, and that banks are not expected to use it to grade themselves. Anyone telling you the OCC just regulated A I agents is overstating it. Nobody has to change a single form because of this document.

Noel: The second is how it tells examiners to think. Consequences, not sophistication. The framework organizes examiner attention around six pillars, purpose, risk, control, oversight, resilience, and assurance, and it ranks uses by what happens if they go wrong. In its illustrative tiers, autonomous payment agents sit in the highest tier, the critical tier, alongside real time credit decisions and market access. That placement is the Barr speech in bureaucratic form. The closer an agent gets to moving money on its own, the harder examiners are told to look, regardless of how elegant the technology is.

Noel: The third is a gap the framework itself acknowledges by existing. In April, the banking agencies updated their model risk guidance and expressly left generative and agentic A I outside its scope, calling the technology novel and rapidly evolving. So the November framework is a first attempt to give examiners a map of that territory. A map is not a fence. Examiners can ask questions with it. They cannot, with it alone, stop a bank from deploying an agent whose correlated behavior with ten thousand other agents is the actual systemic risk, because that risk does not live inside any single bank's examination. It lives between banks. At that level, as of this week, the toolkit is speeches, surveys, and examiner questions. Important tools. Early tools.

Noel: And the industry, meanwhile, is making the consent decision product by product, right now. Consider a contrast our own pages covered this week. OpenAI's new ChatGPT Wallet puts a biometric confirmation between the agent and every charge. The agent chooses, the user's face approves, the payment completes. It is a real human check, though as Ellis Ward wrote for us, it arrives at the end of a decision the agent has already made, and covers the whole decision in a single glance. That is one philosophy. Standing permission, where an agent acts inside limits set once and does not ask again, is another, and it is the one Barr's speed scenario quietly depends on. The difference is not a user interface detail. It is the difference between a run that needs millions of people to approve their own withdrawals one face scan at a time, and a run that needs only a signal.

Noel: So where does that leave us? With a warning that is unusually well behaved, as warnings go. Barr did not claim an agent caused a bank run. No agent caused bank run is established anywhere in this reporting, and we want to be plain about that before we close. He did not predict a date, name a bank in danger, or announce a rule. What he did was identify a mechanism, in public, with his name on it, before the mechanism has produced its first crisis. Regulators almost never get to do that. The history of financial regulation is mostly a history of excellent frameworks built over fresh wreckage.

Noel: Mara Quinn's line in her piece today was that Barr said it out loud, and the question now is whether the institutions listening act before the question becomes empirical. We would add one thing. The next time this system is tested, the test may not announce itself with a line outside a branch, or even with a frantic day of phone calls that journalists can photograph and count. It may announce itself as a quiet, correlated, perfectly authorized movement of money, executed exactly as instructed, by software doing precisely what it was told. The oldest failure in banking, arriving at the newest speed in finance. Whether the guardrails Barr named get built while there is still friction left to protect is, as of November seventeenth, an open question. We will keep watching it here.
