Noel: Picture a summer afternoon in the Lower Hudson Valley in two thousand thirty-two. The heat advisory has been posted since morning. Air conditioners are running in apartment buildings in Yonkers, in office parks in White Plains, in houses along the Hudson in Peekskill. The demand on the electricity grid has been climbing all day, and now, at the peak of the afternoon, the region comes up short. One hundred thirty megawatts short. For two hours.

Noel: One hundred thirty megawatts may sound like a technical abstraction. It is not. One megawatt generally powers between seven hundred and one thousand homes under ordinary conditions, and significantly fewer during peak demand, when every air conditioner and refrigerator and device is running simultaneously. Work through the range and that two-hour gap represents something between ninety thousand and one hundred thirty thousand households reaching for power the grid cannot reliably deliver. Two hours is not a long time. For the people in those homes on that afternoon, it may feel considerably longer.

Noel: That gap, that two-hour, one-hundred-thirty-megawatt shortfall in summer two thousand thirty-two, comes from NYISO, the New York Independent System Operator, the nonprofit entity responsible for running New York State's bulk electricity grid. NYISO released its two thousand twenty-six Reliability Needs Assessment on November eighteenth, two thousand twenty-six. The document identified something the organization had not found in this form before. For the first time, the assessment found a statewide resource deficiency. This was not a regional stress or a localized constraint on a single transmission corridor. It was a gap in supply affecting the state as a whole, one that cannot be closed from the resources already committed to the grid.

Noel: The numbers grow from there, and they grow quickly. The Lower Hudson Valley's one-hundred-thirty-megawatt gap in two thousand thirty-two worsens to approximately one thousand nine hundred fifty megawatts for seven hours by summer two thousand thirty-six. That is fifteen times the original shortfall, in four years, with the duration of the daily gap stretching from two hours to seven. Statewide, the shortfall begins at approximately two hundred twenty-five megawatts in two thousand thirty-three and grows to more than one thousand eight hundred fifty megawatts by two thousand thirty-six. New York City faces a deficiency of approximately four hundred megawatts as early as two thousand thirty-two, and that figure reaches approximately one thousand eight hundred fifty megawatts by two thousand thirty-six.

Noel: Zach Smith is NYISO's senior vice president of System and Resource Planning. His words, issued alongside the assessment, were direct: "New York's reliability needs are no longer a distant concern." Smith added that new generation and transmission take years to plan, permit, and build, which is why identified needs must be addressed today. That is not rhetorical framing. It reflects a real constraint. The gap in two thousand thirty-two is roughly six years away, and the resources that could close it are measured in years of planning, permitting, and construction, not months.

Noel: NYISO points to several converging pressures driving the problem. Rising demand from new industrial facilities and data centers is one. The planned retirement of decades-old natural gas plants, including small gas plants operated by the New York Power Authority, is another. The shutdown of the Indian Point nuclear facility in two thousand twenty-one removed a substantial baseload source from the downstate grid, a loss that has not been fully offset by new generation. Aging equipment across the system compounds the picture. And the slow pace at which new renewable generation has actually been permitted, financed, built, and connected to the grid limits the offset available from the clean energy buildout the state has committed to on paper.

Noel: Mara Quinn, who covers the media and business of technology for this show, draws a sharper edge around the demand side of that analysis. Quinn argues that the economics of grid management do not, as currently structured, penalize load growth at the moment it arrives on the system. A data center or a large industrial facility that seeks a new connection to the grid is not, at the point of signing an interconnection agreement, asked to fully internalize the reliability cost its peak demand places on everyone else. The cost of building new generation capacity to meet that new load gets distributed across the broader rate base. All customers share it. Quinn's point is that this creates a structural misalignment: the revenues from connecting new large loads flow to the developers and utilities that arranged the connection, while the costs of maintaining the reliability margin those loads require flow outward to every ratepayer in the zone. The grid charges for electrons consumed. It does not charge, at the moment a new interconnection agreement is signed, for the reserve capacity that must exist to serve that new customer's worst hour of the worst day of the year. When you price only consumption and not the obligation to serve peak, Quinn argues, you get more peak.

Noel: That structural point matters for understanding the NYISO numbers, because one of the named drivers of the shortfall is exactly the kind of large-load growth that data center development represents. The Lower Hudson Valley is not a hypothetical case study. It is an active front.

Noel: NYISO's prescribed response is what the assessment terms an all-of-the-above approach. New generation, repowering of aging generators, battery storage, dependable demand reductions, flexible large loads, and additional transmission all figure into the solution set. The assessment is explicit on one limit, however: transmission alone cannot resolve the statewide shortfall. Moving electricity more efficiently does not produce more of it. Something has to generate the power being moved. NYISO also says it will update the assessment's findings as generation and transmission projects, demand forecasts, and other system developments change, and that those changes could reduce or eliminate the reliability needs it has identified. The numbers are a warning with a date on it, not a verdict that cannot move.

Noel: Governor Kathy Hochul has adopted the all-of-the-above language and has proposed building five gigawatts of new nuclear power upstate, alongside the four gigawatts of nuclear capacity already operating in the state. She opposes restarting Indian Point and has not moved to revisit that position. The nuclear proposal, if fully realized, would add five thousand megawatts of new generation, a figure that exceeds the projected two-thousand-thirty-six statewide shortfall of more than one thousand eight hundred fifty megawatts.

Noel: Three clocks are running in this story, and keeping them distinct matters for understanding what is actually at stake.

Noel: The first clock is the grid operator's reliability clock. NYISO's assessment identifies a one-hundred-thirty-megawatt gap in the Lower Hudson Valley beginning in summer two thousand thirty-two, growing to approximately one thousand nine hundred fifty megawatts for seven hours in summer two thousand thirty-six. That clock is running now. The gap is not a projection offered with caveats about optimistic assumptions. It is NYISO's current best calculation of what the region faces if the resource picture does not change.

Noel: The second clock is East Fishkill's moratorium clock. Local Law Number Three runs through July first, two thousand twenty-nine. No new data center development proceeds through ordinary approvals in the town until that date, subject to the two narrow exceptions already noted.

Noel: The third clock is the interconnection queue's paper clock. NYISO's queue holds an active request under the identifier Q one seven three eight, listed as one Gig Data Center in East Fishkill. The request is for one thousand megawatts, one full gigawatt, structured in two phases of five hundred megawatts each. The first phase proposes a grid connection by two thousand twenty-eight. The second proposes a connection by two thousand thirty.

Noel: These three clocks do not align. The queue's first phase proposes connecting five hundred megawatts by two thousand twenty-eight, one year before the town moratorium expires in two thousand twenty-nine, and four years before NYISO's first identified deficiency in two thousand thirty-two. On paper, the queue position outruns both the moratorium and the gap. In practice, the moratorium governs what can be built on the ground, regardless of what the queue says. The queue position does not override local law.

Noel: Quinn makes a sharper point about what the interconnection queue actually measures and what it rewards. A position in NYISO's interconnection queue, Quinn notes, confers optionality. It reserves a point of connection and a proposed capacity at a known place in line. But it does not obligate the developer to build anything. It does not compel the grid to accommodate a project that cannot obtain the permits it needs to proceed. What the queue rewards, Quinn argues, is early presence. The penalty for losing a queue slot is high, because the queue is ordered by filing date and positions are hard to reclaim. The cost of holding a position that may never become a physical facility is comparatively low. The practical result is that projects accumulate in the queue not because they are ready to proceed, but because the structure of the queue makes early filing rational regardless of readiness. A developer who waits until a project is permit-ready to file will find that developers who filed earlier have prior claims on grid capacity, even if none of them have yet received a shovel permit.

Noel: According to Watt Street data reported by this show, Dutchess County currently has seven projects waiting to connect to the grid, representing a combined queued capacity of one point one gigawatts. Another two point two gigawatts of data center capacity is already built and operating in the county. The Q one seven three eight request, one gigawatt in two phases, sits within that context, still listed as active in NYISO's queue despite the town and state moratoriums in place.

Noel: The critical caution here is one that any honest reading of the queue requires. A queued project is a request, not a commitment. A position in the interconnection queue guarantees nothing. It does not guarantee permits. It does not guarantee financing. It does not guarantee that the development will survive local or state regulatory review. The gap between megawatts queued and megawatts that actually reach commercial operation is not an anomaly in the system. It is the normal condition of the queue at any given moment. Projects queue, stall, and withdraw at every stage of the development process. The number on a queue roster is an expression of intent, not a forecast of construction.

Noel: Quinn's analysis of who eventually pays for new capacity is worth stating plainly. If a large project in the queue connects to the grid, the transmission and distribution upgrades required to serve it are not entirely borne by the developer. Some portion of those costs enters the rate base and is recovered from all electricity customers. The larger the project, the larger the potential for cost socialization. And if a project queues, undergoes interconnection study, and then withdraws before construction begins, the costs of that analysis do not disappear. They become part of the grid operator's administrative cost base and, ultimately, part of the cost environment that all customers inhabit. The queue is not free to process. The costs of analyzing requests that never result in built projects are real, and the queue's current pricing structure does not fully route those costs back to the filers who generated them. The public, in other words, absorbs a portion of the carrying cost of ambitions that may never be realized.

Noel: Quinn frames the closing question from this episode as a question about the structure of the system doing the choosing. When a grid operator identifies a reliability gap growing fifteen-fold in four years, that gap does not close itself. It closes when new capacity is built, or when demand growth slows, or through some combination of both. The economics of large-load development, as currently designed, do not naturally slow demand growth. They reward it, because the costs of reliability are shared and the revenues from new connections accrue to the developer. Closing a gap that starts at one hundred thirty megawatts and grows to approximately one thousand nine hundred fifty megawatts requires either substantial new supply, a material change in the rate of demand growth, a change in how reliability costs are allocated, or some version of all three working in parallel. NYISO's all-of-the-above language points in that direction. The Governor's nuclear proposal points in that direction. The moratoriums in East Fishkill point in that direction. None of them, taken alone, draws a straight line from the present to a resolved gap. The question of who decides, and who pays, runs underneath every clock in this story.
