Noel: Now the law itself, because the ASSET Unit is only as real as the statute behind it, and the statute has had an unusual path.

Noel: The RAISE Act began as a bill sponsored by Senator Andrew Gounardes and passed by both chambers in June of twenty twenty five. Governor Kathy Hochul signed it on December nineteenth, twenty twenty five, on the condition that the legislature pass agreed chapter amendments bringing it closer to California's frontier A I law. Those amendments were signed on March twenty seventh, twenty twenty six, and they did not merely adjust the original text. They repealed it and replaced it, and they moved the effective date to January first, twenty twenty seven. So the only version that matters is the amended one, now codified in Article forty four B of the General Business Law.

Noel: Who does it cover? The Act works with three nested definitions, and precision here decides which companies are actually inside the fence.

Noel: A frontier model is a foundation model trained using more than ten to the twenty sixth power integer or floating point operations, counting the original training run and later fine tuning of an earlier model. A frontier developer is anyone who has trained, or started training, such a model. And a large frontier developer, the category carrying the heaviest duties, is a frontier developer whose annual gross revenues, together with its affiliates, exceeded five hundred million dollars in the preceding calendar year. The law reaches only models developed, deployed, or operating in whole or in part in New York State. Reporting on the registration opening has named OpenAI and Anthropic as examples of the labs expected to file.

Noel: What must they do? Three kinds of paper, each with its own clock.

Noel: The first is a frontier A I framework. A large frontier developer must write, follow, and clearly publish on its website a document describing the standards it incorporates, the thresholds it uses to judge whether a model could present a catastrophic risk, the mitigations it applies and how it tests them, how it uses third parties to assess risk, how it secures unreleased model weights, how it identifies and responds to critical safety incidents, and how its internal governance ensures compliance. The framework must be reviewed at least once a year, material changes must be published with a justification, and the Act bars materially false or misleading statements about compliance with it, unless made in good faith and reasonable under the circumstances.

Noel: The second is a transparency report. Every frontier developer, large or not, must publish one before deploying a new or substantially modified frontier model, covering how to contact the developer, the intended uses of the model, and any general restrictions on its use. Large developers must add summaries of their catastrophic risk assessments and their results, and the role third parties played. Large developers must also send the state summaries of catastrophic risk assessments arising from internal use of their models every three months, unless the state agrees to another schedule.

Noel: The third is registration. Before developing, deploying, or operating a frontier model in New York, a large frontier developer must file a disclosure statement identifying the company, its ownership, and its contacts for regulators, and pay a pro rata share of the costs of administering the Act. The filing must be renewed every two years, or sooner when the information materially changes, and operating without a current filing can bring a civil penalty of one thousand dollars a day, plus the assessments owed. Registration runs through an online system called the RAISE Portal, which opened on November tenth, eight days before the ASSET announcement. The first filings are due by January first.

Noel: Then there is the reporting duty, the sharpest clock in the Act. From January first, a frontier developer must report a critical safety incident to the state within seventy two hours of determining that one has occurred, or of learning facts sufficient to establish a reasonable belief that one has occurred. California's law allows fifteen days for the same report. New York chose seventy two hours. And where an incident poses an imminent risk of death or serious physical injury, the clock collapses to twenty four hours, and the report goes to an appropriate authority, including law enforcement.

Noel: What counts as a critical safety incident is defined narrowly, and the narrowness is important. The categories are: unauthorized access to, modification of, or theft of a model's weights that results in death or bodily injury; harm from a catastrophic risk that actually materializes; loss of control of a model causing death or bodily injury; and a model using deceptive techniques against its own developer, outside of testing designed to elicit that behavior, to subvert controls in a way that materially increases catastrophic risk. The state office administering the Act says plainly, on its public reporting page, that these incidents do not encompass all A I related errors, malfunctions, or consumer complaints.

Noel: Underneath all of it sits the definition of catastrophic risk: a foreseeable and material risk that a frontier model will materially contribute to the death of or serious injury to more than fifty people, or more than one billion dollars in damage, through expert level assistance with a chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear weapon, through conduct without meaningful human intervention that would be a cyberattack or certain crimes if a person did it, or through evading the control of its developer or user. Notice the altitude of that threshold. The RAISE Act's core machinery is aimed at the worst cases, not the everyday harms of A I. Those live elsewhere in ASSET's brief.

Noel: Enforcement, and here the story splits in two, because New York has built two different offices around this one law and they do different jobs.

Noel: The penalties the Attorney General can seek are substantial and specific. In a civil action against a large frontier developer that fails to publish or transmit a required document, makes a prohibited statement, fails to report an incident as required, or does not follow its own framework, the Attorney General may recover up to one million dollars for a first violation and up to three million dollars for each subsequent violation. The Act creates no private right of action, so these cases belong to the state or they belong to no one.

Noel: The other side belongs to the Department of Financial Services, in a new office the Governor named on September twenty first: the Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity and Trust, known as DIGIT. DIGIT is the administrator. It receives the disclosure statements, runs the RAISE Portal, collects the incident reports and the quarterly summaries, holds rulemaking authority, assesses the fees, and must produce an annual report to the Governor and the Legislature beginning in twenty twenty eight. DIGIT may share reports and summaries with other government entities, including the Attorney General. So the working picture is a loop: developers file with DIGIT, DIGIT holds the record, and ASSET is the office built to turn that record into cases. Whether that loop runs smoothly nobody can answer yet, because as of November sixteenth DIGIT had published no rules under the Act.

Noel: Now widen the lens, because RAISE is the headline in ASSET's brief, not the whole of it. The SHIELD Act requires companies to maintain reasonable data security practices to prevent data breaches. Article forty seven of the General Business Law requires A I chatbots to make reasonable efforts to detect and address suicidal ideation, and to tell users they are not communicating with a human. And behind all of it, the office holds broad authority to act against unfair, deceptive, and abusive business practices. The frontier model law covers a handful of enormous companies. The rest of ASSET's territory covers the scams, the chatbots, the deepfakes, and the data practices that reach everyone else, including everyone listening in the Hudson Valley.

A standing enforcement unit is permanent machinery pointed at every covered developer at once, and its raw material is not a past scandal but a continuing flow of duties: frameworks that must stay current, reports that must be filed within seventy two hours, registrations that must be renewed. Noel: The second question is evaluation. A frontier A I framework is a self description. Noel: The third question is the threshold problem. The seventy two hour clock is triggered by a defined set of grave events, most of them involving death, bodily injury, or materialized catastrophic harm. The state's own guidance says ordinary errors, malfunctions, and consumer complaints are not critical safety incidents. That is deliberate: the RAISE Act is a catastrophic risk law. Noel: The fourth question is federal. The original RAISE Act was signed about a week after President Trump issued an executive order directing a series of steps, including potential lawsuits against states, aimed at what the order characterized as the most onerous and excessive state A I laws.
