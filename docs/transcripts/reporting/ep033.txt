Welcome to Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. I am Noel. This week we are in Canberra, in the High Court of Australia, where two days of argument ended on Wednesday in the first constitutional challenge to the country's social media age law. Can a government keep children off social media, not by asking parents and not by asking the platforms nicely, but by law, in a country whose constitution protects political communication? The Australian government's lawyers told the seven justices that it can, and that it had to, because the ban was, in their words, necessary to protect developing brains. The challengers told the court that the law does not so much protect children as shut them out, and that it produces what one barrister called the worst possible outcome. The justices have reserved their decision, and a verdict will likely take months. So tonight we are going to do the three things a headline cannot. We will walk through what was actually argued in that courtroom. We will look at how this ban is designed to work, because the design is where a law like this either functions or becomes a slogan. And we will ask why a listener in the Hudson Valley should care about an Australian court case, because the honest answer is that the same argument is already on its way to Albany.

Start with the courtroom. The case is two challenges joined into one. One was brought by the Digital Freedom Project, a Sydney-based rights group led by John Ruddick, a Libertarian member of the New South Wales parliament. The other was brought by Reddit, the American online forum, which objects among other things to being on the government's list at all. Both say the same thing in the end: the ban burdens the freedom of political communication that Australia's constitution implies, because it cuts everyone under sixteen out of the places where a growing share of political conversation now happens. The High Court's own case summary puts the question plainly: whether part four A of the Online Safety Act is invalid, in whole or in part, on that ground. All seven justices sat for the hearing. The state and territory solicitors general intervened on the government's side. And the Communications Minister, Anika Wells, who is a named defendant in the case, sat in court and told reporters outside that the government was defending the rights of children to be protected from harm.

The government's case rested on design, not on content. Its lawyer, Joanna Davidson, spent much of the hearing walking the court through seven risks that she argued are built into the way the platforms themselves work. The list is worth hearing in full, because it is the whole theory of the law in one sentence each: addictive use; harm to sleep; depression; anxiety; suicidal ideation; making disordered eating worse; and intensifying the symptoms of ADHD. Notice what is not on that list. There is no claim about any particular post, video or message. The Commonwealth's argument is that the danger lives in the machinery, the recommended feeds, the endless scroll, the features built to keep a young person on the platform, and that children are, in the government's submission, especially vulnerable to that machinery because their brains are still developing. That phrase, developing brains, is the government's claim, argued by its lawyers, and it is doing a lot of work in this case, so we will keep it where it belongs, attributed to the side that made it. The government's other lawyer, Stephen Lloyd, put the constitutional answer this way: the ban's burden on political communication was, and I am quoting him, indirect in nature and modest in extent. It is worth pausing on that, because it is a concession as much as an argument. The Commonwealth did not stand up and say the ban costs young people nothing. It said the cost is real but small, and worth paying.

Reddit's lawyer, Perry Herzfeld, had two answers. The first was about fit. He told the court the ban was, quoting again, ill-targeted and over broad, and that Reddit, a forum built mostly on text and conversation, should never have been included on the list of ten restricted platforms. The second answer was about what the ban actually does to a determined fourteen year old. Banned from holding an account of her own, she can still pick up an adult's phone, or use an adult's account, and see everything, now without any of the age appropriate settings an account of her own might have carried. That, Herzfeld argued, is the worst possible outcome: children robbed of the ability to view safe content, and still exposed to the harmful kind. He also pushed back on the government's most interesting suggestion. Lloyd had argued that a platform could build a separate, children only version of itself, without the features the Commonwealth calls harmful, and that such a service could be allowed. It would be, Lloyd said, a platform for kids that protects kids. Herzfeld replied that the law as written does not allow children to hold any accounts at all, including restricted ones, so the safe version the government describes is one the government's own law appears to forbid.

The government's answer to the shut out argument is worth hearing too, because it narrows what the ban actually takes. A child in Australia can still open YouTube or TikTok and watch. She can read posts, follow a link, see the speech. What she cannot do is hold the account: no profile of her own, no posting under her own name, and above all no personalized feed assembled from her behavior. On the Commonwealth's telling, that is exactly why the burden is modest. The state has not closed the library; it has taken away the machinery that follows her around inside it. Whether you find that convincing rather depends on whether you think the account is the doorway or the destination, and that, in the end, is the argument the seven justices took away with them.

The Digital Freedom Project's counsel, Adrian Maroya, made the participation argument in its purest form: if young people are protected by the implied freedom, then the modes of communication social media offers are not a distraction from political life, they are part of it. Outside the court, that argument had a face. Noah Jones is sixteen now, a founding member of the Digital Freedom Project. The group's case began last year with two fifteen year olds as plaintiffs, Jones and Macy Neyland; both have since stepped back as plaintiffs, but Jones was at the court on Tuesday, saying that young people should be able to share their views online, otherwise they would be, in his words, spectators in their own world. They should not be shut out, he said. Hold that phrase, because it is the cleanest statement of one side of this case. The other side's cleanest statement is the government's seven risks. Everything the seven justices now have to decide sits between those two sentences: whether the harm done by limiting children's political engagement outweighs the harm done by the platforms themselves.

Now the design, because a court can uphold a law that does not work, and strike down one that does, and the working is a separate question. The ban comes from the Online Safety Amendment Act of twenty twenty-four, which wrote a new part, part four A, into Australia's Online Safety Act. It took effect on the tenth of December last year. Ten platforms are covered: Reddit, Facebook, Instagram, Kick, Snapchat, Threads, TikTok, X, YouTube and Twitch. Just as telling is who is not on the list: Discord, WhatsApp and Messenger are not age restricted, which is why Reddit's inclusion argument has some bite, because the line the government drew is not a line around all social software, it is a line around a particular kind of public, feed driven service. The legal duty does not fall on families. No child and no parent is fined for an underage account. The duty falls entirely on the platforms, which must take what the law calls reasonable steps to prevent Australians under sixteen from creating accounts or keeping the ones they have. If they fail, the penalties run to fines of up to three hundred thousand penalty units, which the eSafety Commissioner's current guidance puts at about one hundred nine million Australian dollars.

Reasonable steps is the hinge of the whole scheme, and the regulator, the eSafety Commissioner, has deliberately left it flexible. The published guidance describes a layered approach, sometimes called successive validation: a platform stacks several age signals in sequence rather than relying on any one check. A simple self declaration of age is not enough on its own. Facial age estimation, behavioral signals from how an account is used, third party age checks, all can play a part. And there is a hard privacy edge to it: a platform may not make a government identity document the only way through. If official ID is offered as one option, a reasonable alternative must always exist, and the information gathered for an age check is supposed to be used for that purpose and then destroyed. Think about what that means in practice, because this is where the business story lives. The law does not ask the platforms to check the ages of children. It asks them to check the ages of everyone, adults included, because you cannot know which accounts belong to under sixteens without forming a view about every account. The cost and the friction land on the whole user base, and a new industry of age assurance providers has grown up to sell the checking.

So, eleven months in, is it working? The honest answer from the government's own numbers is: partly, and less than Canberra hoped. Platforms reported restricting about four point seven million accounts in the first implementation period, by the middle of January, out of a national population of twenty eight million. But the regulator's own evaluation, which is following more than four thousand children and families, found that three months in, the share of ten to fifteen year olds holding at least one account had fallen from just over half to about four in ten, and that half of the children who kept their accounts said no platform had ever asked them to confirm their age. The government has acknowledged that the ban has not been as effective as it hoped; many children, the Associated Press reports, have slipped through the age checks and opened accounts anyway. In June, Canberra moved to strengthen the laws behind the ban, and in September Parliament raised the maximum penalty. The regulator has opened investigations into several of the biggest platforms over alleged non compliance, though as of this week's hearing it had not fined any company. And notice the deeper tension the court case exposes. The government's lawyers argue the platforms are so powerful, and their design so effective at holding young attention, that only a ban will do. The government's own results argue the platforms' age checks can be walked around by a motivated teenager in an afternoon. Both of those things were said, in different rooms, by the same side, and the justices are allowed to notice.

The enforcement has a rhythm worth noticing as well. When the law switched on last December, the platforms moved in bulk, closing or freezing accounts by the million rather than risk the fines, and the regulator followed with more than twenty formal notices demanding to see how the companies were checking ages and what their systems were catching. In other words, reasonable steps stopped being a phrase in a statute and became an audit, repeated on a schedule, with the paperwork to match.

There is one more actor in this story whose incentives are worth naming, and that is the platforms themselves, because the Commonwealth's theory of harm is also a theory about money. The government's case is that these services are built by companies that earn from advertising, and that advertising pays for engagement and retention, which is why the feeds are engineered the way Davidson described. If that is right, then asking the platforms to police themselves was never going to work, and the ban is what a government does when it stops asking. The platforms' counter argument, made through Reddit, is not really about revenue; it is that they are being made responsible for a line they cannot draw cleanly, and punished for drawing it badly, while the services children actually migrate to sit outside the list. Both of those accounts can be true at once, and the compliance departments now being built inside ten very large companies are the evidence that, whatever the court decides, the spending has already happened.

There is a quieter lesson in the numbers for anyone who runs a platform, and this part is our reading of the record rather than the record itself. Australia has twenty eight million people. It is a good market and a small one, the kind of market a global company might once have threatened to leave rather than rebuild its product around a local rule. Nobody left. The companies complained, they lobbied, and in Reddit's case they sued, but they also spent the money, built the checks and closed the accounts. Our analysis, and we label it as ours, is that the cost of compliance looked lower than the cost of fighting the same idea in every capital it might spread to next. That is how a rule written in Canberra becomes a feature request in California: not by force, but by arithmetic.

Which brings us to the export question, and to you. Australia was the first country to do this, and it is no longer the only one moving. Other countries have since adopted or pursued similar age rules; that is the Associated Press and Australian reporting, and it is deliberately vague, because the details differ everywhere. What is not vague is New York. Our state did not copy Australia. The SAFE for Kids Act, signed by Governor Hochul in June of twenty twenty-four, takes a narrower and more American route: it does not ban accounts at all. Instead, it says that unless a platform has determined a user is an adult, or has a parent's verifiable consent, it may not give that user an algorithmically personalized feed, and it may not send them notifications in the nighttime hours. The Attorney General's final rules for the Act were released this past July, and they are a remarkable document, because they try to write the same age assurance problem Australia is wrestling with into measurable standards: methods must be tested and certified every year by an accredited third party; the permitted error rates are strictest for the youngest children, one percent for ages eight to thirteen, loosening as teenagers approach eighteen; at least one method other than government ID must be offered; and the data gathered to check an age must be used for that alone and then deleted or de-identified. The rules apply to platforms where the addictive feed is a significant part of the service, defined as a fifth or more of the time users spend there. They take effect on the twenty fifth of January next year, with penalties of up to five thousand dollars per violation. The Attorney General's office has even priced the technology, estimating age assurance at roughly fifteen to thirty cents per user. New York is not banning the room; it is regulating the machinery inside the room, which is exactly the distinction Australia's government lawyers were drawing from the other direction when they listed those seven design risks in Canberra.

So here is where things stand. In Canberra, the ban stays in force while the seven justices consider it, and a decision is likely months away. If the court finds for the challengers, part four A could be struck down in whole or in part, and the world's first social media age ban would need to be rebuilt or abandoned. If the court finds for the Commonwealth, the model is validated in the hardest forum available, and every parliament and legislature watching, including ours, will read that judgment as a green light to go further than feeds and notifications. Either way, notice what did not happen in that courtroom. Nobody argued that the feeds are harmless. Nobody argued that a thirteen year old and a recommender system are evenly matched. The argument was about who decides, and with what tool, and at whose expense: a parliament with a ban, a court with a constitution, a parent with a consent form, or a platform with a business model. Australia has given its answer, and is now waiting to hear whether its highest court will let the answer stand. We will follow the decision when it comes.
