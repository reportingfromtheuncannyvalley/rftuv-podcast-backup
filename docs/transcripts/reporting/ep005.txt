Noel: That is not inherently corrupt. Standards bodies are almost always made up of industry participants. The internet protocols we still use today were shaped heavily by companies with interests in how the internet worked. Useful infrastructure gets built this way. But it is important to be clear about what is happening, because this standard will determine a lot of things that are not obvious yet.

Noel: Specifically: whose interests get optimized when an agent negotiates on your behalf?

Noel: An agent built on Meta's platform, operating under a credential framework Meta helped design, interacting with businesses using systems Sierra helped configure, is not a neutral party. Noel: The pitch for agent standards is real and legitimate. If there is no common handshake, agents become chaotic. Every website, every retailer, every service provider builds its own system. Noel: The question worth asking is whether the standard being designed is the one that works best for consumers, or the one that works best for the companies designing it. In most cases, you cannot fully know the answer until the standard has been in use long enough to see how it plays out at scale.

Noel: Now the other side of the counter.

Noel: While Meta and Sierra work on agent credentials, website operators are taking matters into their own hands. A growing number of sites are updating their terms of service and their technical configurations to block agent access unless the agent meets conditions the site controls.

Noel: Some of this is understandable. Agents that scrape product data, compare prices in real time, and route purchases away from impulse toward optimization are a direct threat to the revenue models most retail websites rely on. A website built around the assumption that a human is browsing, getting distracted, seeing related products, responding to urgency signals, and maybe buying something unplanned is a very different business from a website that only ever sees agents arriving with precise purchase instructions and no margin for persuasion.

Noel: Retailers have known this was coming. What they are doing now is erecting the equivalent of velvet ropes. Not to keep out all agents, but to keep out agents that do not play by their rules.

Noel: The rules vary. Some sites require agent identification in advance, a registration or certification that lets the site know who it is dealing with. Some set conditions on what data an agent can access. Some charge for access, a licensing fee or a data fee that agents pay to transact. Some block agents entirely until a human opts in from a verified account.

Noel: Each of these is defensible in isolation. You can argue that websites have always had terms of service. You can argue that robot exclusion has been a standard part of web infrastructure since the nineteen ninetys. You can argue that an agent that circumvents a site's access rules is no different from a scraper, and scrapers have been legally contentious for decades.

Noel: What is new is the scale and the commercial intent. Sites are not just protecting data. They are protecting the specific conditions under which money changes hands. They are deciding which agents get to transact on their platform and on what terms. That is not a technical decision. It is a market structure decision.

Noel: And when you put the two sides together, the standard-setters and the gatekeepers, you get a picture that should interest anyone who cares about what happens to the consumer in the middle.

Noel: Your agent, built on a platform governed by a standard designed by Meta and Sierra, arrives at a website that has its own set of requirements for agent access.
