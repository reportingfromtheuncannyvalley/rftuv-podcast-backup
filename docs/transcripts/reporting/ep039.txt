Noel: The pager goes off at three in the morning. That is the oldest ritual in modern computing. Somewhere a system has failed, or thinks it has, and a human being who drew the short rotation wakes up, opens a laptop in the dark, and starts asking the only question that matters at three in the morning: what broke, and where do I look first.

Noel: That job has a name. The people who carry the pager are site reliability engineers, SREs, and the work they do in the first hour of an incident follows a shape that has not changed much in twenty years. An alert fires. Someone triages it: real failure or noise. Someone forms a hypothesis about the root cause. Someone gathers evidence: logs, metrics, the last change anyone shipped. Then, and only then, someone starts fixing things.

Noel: Understand what the rotation really is, because it matters for everything that follows. On call is not a shift. It is a promise that at any hour, on any night, one named person is responsible for the health of the system, and that the responsibility cannot be delegated to a ticket queue or a group inbox. Companies pay people extra to carry that promise because the alternative is discovering, at the worst possible moment, that a failure belonged to everyone and therefore to no one. The pager is heavy because the accountability is the point.

Noel: Start with what was actually announced, because the claims deserve to be separated from the launches. SiliconANGLE reported this week that a company called Resolve A I has built a model called SRE-one, designed to find the root causes of production alerts. Read that description carefully. Not to watch alerts. Not to summarize alerts. To find root causes: the step in the three in the morning workflow where a tired human stares at twelve dashboards and decides which one is lying.

Noel: SiliconANGLE also reported the launch of Agent Investigations from a company called Groundcover: a shared workspace where A I agents and human engineers investigate incidents together. Hold those two launches side by side, because they describe two different futures. In the first, the agent takes the hardest step of the workflow for itself. In the second, the agent works the case alongside the engineer, in a workspace built for both of them. One is a replacement story. The other is a partnership story. The industry is telling both at the same time, in the same week, sometimes in the same product category.

Noel: And then there is the claim that frames all of it. Quartz reported this week that Google says autonomous A I agents are outpacing human response. Outpacing. That is the word to sit with, and we will come back to it, because a claim about speed is only as good as what was timed.

Noel: First, though, the work itself. What does an on call agent actually do at three in the morning?

Noel: Strip the job down to its sequence and it sounds mechanical, which is exactly why vendors believe software can do it. An alert is a structured thing: a service name, a threshold crossed, a timestamp. Triage is a classification problem: does this alert correspond to users being hurt, or is it a sensor complaining about itself? Root cause analysis is a search problem: across the logs, the metrics, and the recent changes, find the event that explains the symptoms. Evidence gathering is retrieval. On paper, every step of that sequence is something a language model wrapped in tools can attempt, and attempt without waking anyone up.

Noel: Walk the sequence slowly, the way it actually happens, and the mechanical picture gets more complicated. The alert names a symptom, and the symptom is almost never the disease. A database alert fires because a deploy an hour earlier changed a query pattern. A latency alert fires because a different team entirely shipped a feature that doubled traffic to one endpoint. The first piece of evidence every experienced engineer checks is the change log, not because changes are usually guilty, but because the timeline is the only honest narrator an incident has. Then come the comparisons: what does normal look like at this hour, on this day, against last week. Then the narrowing: which systems are healthy, which are merely quiet, which are silent because they are dead. None of these steps is mystical. All of them are context: the accumulated knowledge of what this particular system is supposed to look like when it is well.

Noel: That is why anyone who has carried a pager can tell you where the sequence stops being mechanical. The alert that fires is rarely the thing that broke. The dashboard that screams loudest is rarely the cause. The experienced engineer at three in the morning is not running a checklist; she is running a memory of every previous incident, every known weakness in the system, every change a teammate mentioned in passing on Tuesday. The root cause call is a judgment made under uncertainty with incomplete evidence, and the cost of a wrong call is not abstract. Chase the wrong cause and the real failure keeps burning while the team works on a healthy system.

Noel: That is the standard the new tools have to meet, and it is worth being precise about how the two launches this week position themselves against it. A model built to find root causes, like Resolve A I's SRE-one as SiliconANGLE describes it, is aimed directly at the judgment step. It is not offering to fetch the logs faster. It is offering the conclusion. A shared investigation workspace, like Groundcover's Agent Investigations, makes a more modest and in some ways more interesting claim: that the investigation itself becomes a joint activity, the agent assembling evidence and proposing lines of inquiry, the engineer steering, correcting, and deciding. The human moves from driver to editor of the investigation. Whether that is a smaller change than it sounds is one of the questions this episode is asking.

Noel: Notice, too, what the partnership version quietly admits. If the winning design is a workspace where the agent proposes and the human disposes, then the industry's own product designers are telling us the judgment step is not ready to be handed over whole. The agent is at its best, in that design, doing the parts of the night that were always retrieval: gathering the logs, lining up the timeline, drafting the summary nobody wants to write at four in the morning. That is real value. The three in the morning shift is full of work that is beneath the engineer doing it, and every incident responder alive has a list of hours they would gladly give back. The argument of this episode is not that the agents should stay out of the incident room. It is that the distance between assisting the investigation and owning the conclusion is the distance that matters, and this week's launches sit on opposite sides of it.

Noel: Now the speed claim, because it is the claim that will be quoted in every boardroom version of this story. Google says autonomous agents are outpacing human response, as Quartz reported it. Outpacing at what, exactly? The reporting available this week does not settle the measurement, and the measurement is the whole story. There are at least three races hidden inside that one word, and they have different winners.

Noel: The first race is time to first action, and it is easy for a machine to win: an agent does not need to wake up, find its glasses, or wait for a laptop to boot. If the race is measured from alert to first keystroke, the human loses every time, and the loss tells you almost nothing. The second race is time to correct diagnosis, and it is the one that matters, because a fast wrong answer in an incident is worse than a slow right one. The third race is time to resolution, and it depends on everything downstream: permissions, approvals, the blast radius of the fix, the rollback that has to be staged before anyone touches production. A claim that agents are outpacing humans, without the metric attached, is a claim about a race whose finish line has not been shown to us.

Noel: There is a second thing the speed claim leaves out, and it is the thing incident veterans will name first: false positives and wrong actions. A human on call who is unsure can wait, watch, and gather one more piece of evidence before touching production. Waiting is a skill. It looks like doing nothing, and it is often the most valuable thing the person on call does all night, because systems under stress sometimes recover, sometimes reveal themselves, and always punish the impatient. An automated system acting at machine speed on a wrong root cause does not just waste the hour; it can make the incident worse, changing the system while the real failure continues, and leaving the next investigator a crime scene that the first investigator rearranged. None of this is an argument that agents should never touch production systems. It is an argument that the honest comparison is not agent speed against human speed. It is agent judgment, at agent speed, against human judgment at human speed, scored on outcomes. That study, as of this week, has not been published by anyone.

Noel: Now the recursion, which is where this story stops being a product story and becomes a systems story. The systems these agents will investigate are increasingly built and run by other agents. Code is written by agents. Deployments are assembled by pipelines no single human fully reviews. Configuration changes arrive at a pace that has already outrun the incident review process at many companies. So the on call agent of the near future will spend a growing share of its nights investigating failures caused, in whole or in part, by other automated systems acting on instructions nobody remembers giving. The investigator and the suspect are the same kind of thing.

Noel: Think about what that does to the change log, the honest narrator. When the changes themselves are machine generated, at machine volume, the timeline stops being a short list a human can hold in her head and becomes another haystack that takes a machine to search. The recursion feeds itself: automation creates the volume that requires automation to investigate. That is not a conspiracy and it is not a scandal. It is the ordinary logic of scale, the same logic that automated the watching in the first place. But it does mean the question of who understands the system, as a whole, in a way that fits inside one mind, gets harder every quarter. The incident shift was one of the last places where that whole system knowledge was exercised under pressure, by a person, as a job. What replaces that exercise is an open question, and it is bigger than any product launch.

Noel: The security world is already living in that recursion, and it is the parallel case worth watching. BizTech Magazine described this week the rise of the agentic security operations center: A I agents joining the security team, watching networks, triaging alerts, hunting intrusions. A security operations center is an incident shift that never ends, and its alerts increasingly concern the behavior of software acting on its own. Agents watching agents, on both sides of the wall, because the volume of machine activity has passed the point where human attention can be the first filter. The SRE story and the security story are the same story told in two departments: the work of watching systems has become too voluminous for the watchers, so the watchers are being automated.

Noel: Security also offers the cautionary version of the speed argument, a decade early. Automated response in security has always faced the same fork: an agent that can isolate a machine in seconds can also isolate the wrong machine in seconds, and the history of the field is full of defenses that became outages. The security teams learned to grade autonomy by consequence: watch everything, recommend freely, act alone only where the action is reversible and the confidence is high. Nothing in this week's incident response launches suggests that lesson has been forgotten. Nothing in the reporting shows it being quoted, either.

Noel: There is a testing corollary, and it deserves one paragraph. WIRED reported recently on what it called the Mirror Dimension: a fake internet Google built for testing Chrome agents, a copy of the web where agents can act and fail safely. Keep it in its proper place in this story. It is the testing side of the same shift: if agents are going to work real shifts on real systems, somebody has to build the practice field. The gap between the practice field and the three in the morning pager is where the next year of this industry will be spent.

Noel: Which brings us to the question the launches do not answer, the one that will be waiting in the first serious postmortem. When the on call agent's root cause call is wrong, whose postmortem is it?

Noel: A postmortem is not really a technical document. It is an accountability document. It records who decided what, on what evidence, and what the organization will change so the decision goes better next time. The modern postmortem is famously blameless toward the individual who made the call, and rigorous about the conditions that made the wrong call likely: the missing dashboard, the ambiguous alert, the runbook nobody updated. That culture was built for human decision makers, and it works because the human can sit in the room, reconstruct what they saw, and say what would have changed their mind. The pager, for all its cruelty, was an accountability device as much as a notification device: it named the human who owned the night.

Noel: An agent cannot own a night in that sense. It cannot be interviewed in the review. It cannot explain what it was thinking in terms the organization can act on, beyond the trace of what it did. So the accountability does not move to the agent. It moves to whoever deployed the agent, set its permissions, chose its thresholds, and decided which classes of incident it may handle alone and which ones still wake a human. The postmortem of the agent era will be written about the humans who configured the agent, and it will be written the first time an automated root cause call, acted on at machine speed, turns a bad night into a historic one. The most important incident document of the next few years may be a permissions file: the list, written in advance by people who were awake and calm, of everything the agent was allowed to do at three in the morning without asking. When the review comes, that list is the testimony.

The direction is not in doubt. What is in doubt, and what the vendors' own claims do not yet establish, is the judgment: whether the thing that wakes up instantly also knows, at three in the morning, which dashboard is lying.
