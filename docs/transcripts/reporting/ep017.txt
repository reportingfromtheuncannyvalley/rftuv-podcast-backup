That experience, which most citizens dread, was always inefficient. What has changed is that tighter budgets and shrinking workforces are making it harder to sustain even at that slow pace. Agents are arriving in the public sector, as payments puts it, as a structural response to a workforce that can no longer keep up with demand.

Noel: That framing matters. The question, in payments's telling, is no longer whether governments should deploy agents. Noel: A World Economic Forum article, described by payments as offering the first systematic framework for assessing where governments should start with agentic A I, maps seventy core government functions against two criteria: the potential for agentic A I to add public value and the complexity of deploying it responsibly. Appointment management, document validation, and public information provision come out particularly well. payments describes these as high volume, rule-based functions where agents can make a visible difference quickly and without excessive risk. That risk characterization is the article's assessment, tied specifically to those function types, not a general claim about government agents.

Noel: The adoption numbers are striking and the caution that goes with them is equally striking, so I am going to carry them together the way the reporting does. Noel: Manuel Kilian, managing director for the Global Government Technology Centre in Berlin, told the World Economic Forum that agents allow governments to move from automating individual tasks to delivering entire outcomes. That path is unglamorous. It is also the one the evidence points toward.

Noel: Ellis Ward's lens on the readiness list is worth sitting with here. Appointment management, document validation, public information provision. But that same quality means an error at the front door reaches everyone, quickly, in the government's own voice. The functions that are easiest to automate are also the ones that touch the most residents.

Noel: Three live deployments come up in payments's coverage of a Route Fifty article on the Urban Institute's new playbook for state and local agentic A I adoption. I want to carry each one carefully, because the figures matter and so do their limits.

Noel: Virginia ran a pilot program that used agentic A I to scan regulatory documents. The reporting, attributed to Route Fifty's coverage with the underlying initiative credited to Virginia's Office of Regulatory Management, says the scan uncovered more than one point four billion dollars in annual savings and reduced permit and license processing times by nearly eighty percent. Those are the figures as reported. I am not presenting them as independently audited results. The framing is savings uncovered or identified, and processing times reduced.

Noel: Utah has an autonomous system handling routine prescription renewals in partnership with a health platform called Doctronic. Physicians agreed with ninety one percent of the A I's decisions. Noel: In Boston, Chief Information Officer Santiago Garces integrated agentic A I into the city's open data portal using an Anthropic model context protocol server, as payments reports it, to analyze city data and inform policymaking in real time. Garces and Anthropic are named in the reporting.

Noel: Ellis Ward's question for each of these is: where does the error surface, and who catches it? In Virginia, a misread regulation makes it into the scan. In Utah, a renewal the physician would have flagged differently. In Boston, an analysis a policymaker treats as ground truth when it should be treated as a starting point. The deployments are real and the results are notable. The governance design that makes errors findable and correctable is what determines whether those results hold.

Noel: The second payments piece published October nineteenth is titled "A I Agents Could Fill Treasury's Looming Talent Gap." When I pulled that piece in full, it is not about the United States Treasury. It is not about a government deployment at all. Noel: The workforce problem in corporate treasury is about people who have spent twenty or thirty years making sure the machinery of a company's finances actually runs. And they are getting ready to retire.

Noel: Katherine Weislogel, head of Commercial Payments Client Success at Fifth Third, spoke to payments C E O Karen Webster about this. The question being asked, as Weislogel frames it, was whether A I could help keep the department running.

Noel: That distinction matters. payments frames the immediate opportunity for businesses facing the loss of experienced employees as getting the work done, not the longer argument about what machines might eventually be trusted to do with money.

Noel: Weislogel described a payment traveling through roughly five separate steps, each potentially involving different people, technologies, and decision points. For years, experienced employees have served as the connective tissue holding those processes together. An agent capable of moving a process forward has to communicate with all five parts of the workflow, otherwise companies end up with faster individual tasks and the same bottlenecks between them.

Noel: She also described the account onboarding problem. A customer submits documents through a bank portal, only to discover the information does not necessarily follow the account through the rest of the process. Weislogel described a different experience, one where the information submitted at the beginning stays with the account as it moves toward implementation. She said, and this is a brief quotation: "The next time I should hear from the bank is when my account number is live." payments says the difference could be measured in days versus hours. Agents can collect information, coordinate workflows, move documents, and advance processes while leaving the actual financial decisions to authorized employees. That boundary is exactly where the piece puts it.

Noel: On the question of authority, Weislogel described what Karen Webster called a dimmer switch, rather than a traditional on-off switch. An agent might be allowed to analyze cash positions, prepare forecasts, or recommend an action. Giving it permission to initiate or execute a payment is a different decision, one that raises questions about fraud, liquidity, entitlements, and reconciliation. Even looking eighteen to twenty four months ahead, Weislogel does not expect businesses to simply hand money movement to A I agents. Human oversight, verification, and spot checks remain part of the process. An agent acting on incomplete information or poorly defined parameters, she noted, can create problems much faster than a manual process.

Noel: payments's framing of where this leads is worth quoting as a frame: the first meaningful return on agentic A I may not be an autonomous treasury department. It may be a treasury department that can keep running when the people who built its processes are no longer there.

Noel: Now bring that logic into government, with one additional factor the private sector does not carry. A resident cannot take their records request to a competitor. The staffing gap motive is the same. The stakes attached to getting it wrong are different in kind.

Noel: Ellis Ward frames it this way: a staffing gap pitch changes what good deployment actually means. The work is not replacing a person with a model. If that documentation work is not done, the agent inherits the gaps, not the knowledge. The speed of the new counter is real. What it delivers depends entirely on what you put into it before you open the door.
