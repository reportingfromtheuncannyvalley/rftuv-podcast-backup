Noel: Welcome to Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. I am Noel. What is a company worth when its customers can stop paying simply by stopping typing?

Noel: That question sits at the center of one of the most consequential financial documents in the artificial intelligence industry. Anthropic, the company behind Claude, has filed to go public. Its registration statement describes extraordinary revenue growth, computing expenses, customer relationships, and corporate control. But another story is developing around that document. It concerns what investors might pay for the company, and whether expectations surrounding artificial intelligence have begun to change.

Noel: On Friday, November sixth, Axios reported that unnamed people familiar with Anthropic's offering described a downward reset in valuation expectations. A day later, Ellis Ward examined that reporting and the company's disclosures for Reporting from the Uncanny Valley. Today's Midday Briefing covered the immediate developments. Here, we are going deeper. What does the company disclose? What does the market infer? And what happens when those stories diverge? The registration statement, known as an S one, was filed on October twenty-sixth and amended on November second. It contains no proposed offering price range. In late October, I T Pro reported that valuations between one point five trillion and two trillion dollars were under discussion. At the upper end, that would exceed the one point eight trillion dollar public debut of SpaceX earlier this year. But those figures are reported expectations, not valuations established by Anthropic's filing, and not prices at which investors have agreed to purchase shares.

Noel: This is where the business press becomes part of the story. Mara Quinn, whose perspective focuses on media and the business of technology, offers a useful framework. A regulatory filing discloses information under formal obligations. Financial journalism interprets that information, gathers expectations from people involved in a transaction, and explains what those expectations might mean. Both have value. They perform different functions. The problem begins when a reported possibility is repeated until audiences treat it as an established fact. A valuation discussed privately is not an offering price. An offering price is not a sustained market price. And market value is not cash sitting in a company's bank account. Those distinctions matter when the numbers reach trillions.

Noel: To understand the valuation debate, begin with what Anthropic reported. In twenty twenty-three, revenue was forty-one million dollars. In twenty twenty-four, it reached three hundred eighty-six million dollars. In twenty twenty-five, revenue climbed to four point five eight five billion dollars. And in the first six months of twenty twenty-six alone, Anthropic reported sixteen point two eight six billion dollars. The company moved from tens of millions in annual revenue to billions, then reported more than sixteen billion dollars in half a year. In the second quarter of twenty twenty-six, revenue grew one thousand three hundred sixty-eight percent compared with the corresponding quarter a year earlier. These figures help explain why investors might contemplate an extraordinary valuation. But revenue growth does not tell us how securely money will continue arriving, how much must be spent to generate it, or how much value will ultimately belong to shareholders.

Noel: Those questions lead to four risks disclosed by the filing. The first is the structure of Anthropic's revenue. Eighty-three percent of revenue during the first half of twenty twenty-six came from consumption-based fees. Customers pay according to their use of the technology, measured in tokens. A token is a unit of text processed by an artificial intelligence model. More work generally means more tokens consumed. When usage increases, spending can rise rapidly. When usage decreases, spending can fall. That differs from a business where most customers pay fixed annual subscriptions regardless of how frequently they use the product. Consumption pricing lets customers pay for work performed and providers participate directly in growing demand. But it also exposes the provider to changes in customer behavior. A customer does not necessarily need to cancel a relationship to reduce spending. It may simply process fewer requests. A company can remain an Anthropic customer while sending substantially less work through Claude. That is the significance of our opening question. What is a company worth when its customers can stop paying by stopping typing? The answer depends partly on how consistently customers find reasons to continue.

Noel: Anthropic's reported net revenue retention was approximately nine hundred percent as of September thirtieth, twenty twenty-six. That indicates extraordinary expansion in spending among existing customers under the company's reported calculation. But retention measures what happened over a particular period. It does not guarantee future spending. Nine hundred percent does not mean customers must spend nine times as much forever. Nor does it mean future spending cannot contract. And extraordinary expansion creates difficult comparisons. The faster a business grows, the more revenue it must generate to maintain the same percentage growth rate.

Noel: That is arithmetic, not a prediction about Anthropic. Another filing detail makes the relationship between usage and revenue especially interesting. Anthropic says Claude Opus five point five reduced customer costs per task by approximately forty percent compared with Opus five. For customers, that can be compelling. They can potentially accomplish comparable work at lower cost. For Anthropic, the revenue implications depend on customer behavior. If lower costs encourage substantially more activity, consumption may increase. If customers perform the same work more efficiently, spending on that work may decline. Both possibilities follow from the pricing structure. The filing does not establish which will dominate. This is a central tension in artificial intelligence economics. Making technology more efficient can increase its usefulness while changing what customers pay for each unit of useful work. Efficiency is an engineering achievement. Revenue is a commercial outcome. The relationship is not automatic.

Noel: Now consider the second risk. Concentration. Anthropic reported that two customers each accounted for twelve percent of revenue in twenty twenty-five. For the nine months ending September thirtieth, twenty twenty-six, the five largest customers accounted for approximately twenty-five percent of revenue. No single customer exceeded ten percent during that later period. These figures describe different reporting periods and should not be collapsed into one snapshot. But they show why the composition of Anthropic's customer base matters. The company also reported more than eleven thousand nine hundred customers spending at least one hundred thousand dollars annually. That is a substantial population of high-spending customers. Yet having many customers does not eliminate dependence on especially large accounts. A business can be broad and concentrated simultaneously. The consequential question is what happens when large customers change purchasing decisions. Do they expand usage, optimize spending, or diversify suppliers? The filing describes past revenue distribution. It cannot answer every future purchasing decision.

Noel: Concentration becomes more complicated when we examine how Anthropic reaches customers. Approximately forty-eight percent of revenue from January through September twenty twenty-six was routed through Amazon and Alphabet's Google. That figure describes distribution channels. It does not mean Amazon and Google were necessarily the ultimate purchasers of every dollar routed through them. But these relationships occupy a significant position in Anthropic's commercial infrastructure. And they are unusually layered. The filing identifies Amazon and Google as distribution partners, infrastructure vendors, and investors. They provide routes through which Anthropic's products reach customers. They participate in the computing infrastructure on which Anthropic depends. And they hold financial interests in the company. Anthropic explicitly identifies this combination as a potential conflict of interest. That is not an accusation of improper conduct. It acknowledges that different commercial roles can create competing incentives. A company purchasing infrastructure wants dependable service and favorable economics. A distribution partner has its own commercial priorities. An investor wants its investment to grow. When the same organizations occupy several positions, decisions become complicated.

Noel: Jan Karstens, chief technology officer of Avantra, told I T Pro that enterprises building critical workflows around a single artificial intelligence supplier face operational exposure if model access changes or commercial relationships shift. His warning concerns customers dependent on a provider. But the same structural concern applies to Anthropic's dependence on cloud infrastructure partners. Anthropic reports managing computing capacity as one fungible pool across Amazon Web Services Trainium, Google tensor processing units, NVIDIA graphics processing units, and AMD graphics processing units. That represents several computing technologies. It does not mean every commercial relationship is interchangeable. Hardware diversity and commercial independence are different things. A company may have several technical options while relying heavily on particular organizations for distribution, infrastructure, or financing.

Noel: This brings us to the third risk. Commitments. Anthropic reported eight point nine four five billion dollars in compute and infrastructure expenses during the first half of twenty twenty-six. In the second quarter, revenue grew one thousand three hundred sixty-eight percent year over year. Compute costs grew three hundred percent over the same period. Revenue therefore grew substantially faster than compute costs in that comparison. That is important. But it does not establish that Anthropic's entire infrastructure burden has disappeared or that future economics are settled. Eight point nine four five billion dollars remains enormous spending over six months. And infrastructure commitments extend to twenty forty-five. Consider those time horizons. Consumption-based revenue responds to what customers do now. Infrastructure commitments can extend across decades. The business must manage demand that can change alongside obligations that may persist. This is why a downward reset in valuation expectations matters even when it does not directly alter existing contracts. A market valuation reflects what investors believe a company is worth. An infrastructure commitment arises from commercial arrangements. The two are economically connected but legally and operationally distinct. If investors revise expectations, contractual commitments do not automatically shrink. The filing does not establish that Anthropic cannot meet its commitments. It establishes that they exist and extend far beyond the immediate offering. This matters when evaluating a company growing at extraordinary speed. The numbers support recognition of strong demand while raising questions about its durability. Both observations can be true. A business may have remarkable commercial momentum and significant long-term obligations.

Noel: Another figure adds perspective. Anthropic reported trailing twelve-month revenue per employee exceeding six point six million dollars as of September thirtieth, twenty twenty-six. A year earlier, it was one point four million dollars. That represents an enormous increase in reported revenue relative to employee count. But revenue per employee is not profit per employee. It does not subtract computing expenses, infrastructure costs, or other expenditures. And it cannot establish how much cash a company ultimately retains. The figure is useful when we understand both what it measures and what it excludes.

Noel: That brings us to the fourth risk. Governance. Anthropic's filing describes a structure separating economic participation from substantial control over corporate decisions. The co-founders collectively control fifty point one percent of total voting power through a single Class F share held in a founder limited liability company. A Long-Term Benefit Trust controls a majority of the board through Class T shares. Public Class A shareholders receive full economic participation and one vote per share. But their practical governance influence is limited by the control structure. For prospective investors, that distinction matters. Owning shares does not necessarily provide proportionate influence over corporate direction. Investors can participate financially while holding relatively little power to change leadership or priorities. That arrangement may appeal to founders seeking to preserve a long-term mission against short-term market pressure. It may concern investors who prefer greater accountability through shareholder voting. Those are different assessments of the same disclosed structure. The filing goes further. Anthropic states that its duty to balance stockholder interests against its public benefit purpose may conflict with financial interests over the short, medium, or long term. That is a direct acknowledgment of potential tension. It tells prospective investors that maximizing financial returns is not necessarily the only objective guiding corporate decisions. This does not establish that Anthropic will disregard shareholders. It means investors are warned that governing commitments may sometimes point in different directions. The public benefit purpose and shareholder financial interests may align. They may also conflict. And because authority is concentrated, public shareholders have limited means of resolving that conflict through ordinary voting power. This detail can disappear beneath an enormous valuation headline. A prospective investor might hear two trillion dollars and think primarily about growth. But the filing asks another question. Who makes decisions when mission, commercial relationships, infrastructure obligations, and shareholder interests collide? Revenue growth alone cannot answer that. It requires understanding control.

Noel: Return now to the reporting that brought us here. Axios described a downward reset in valuation expectations, according to unnamed people familiar with the offering. I T Pro previously reported discussions between one point five trillion and two trillion dollars. The filing contains no price range. Those statements must remain separate. The reported reset does not establish the eventual offering price. The earlier reported range does not establish an official valuation. And extraordinary revenue growth does not prove either expectation correct.

Noel: This is where Mara Quinn's media and technology lens becomes useful. Business journalism does more than transmit financial information. It creates public narratives around that information. A filing may contain thousands of details, but audiences encounter them through headlines, explanations, interviews, and comparisons. One story emphasizes revenue growth. Another highlights infrastructure expenses. Another focuses on valuation. Another examines founder control. Each may describe something real while omitting much of the surrounding picture. Serious financial reporting preserves the boundaries between evidence and interpretation. That matters particularly when unnamed sources describe private expectations. Anonymous sourcing can provide valuable insight into negotiations and changing sentiment. But anonymity limits what audiences can independently assess about a source's position, incentives, and access. Readers know what Axios reported. They do not know the identities of those supplying the expectations. That uncertainty belongs in the story. It should not disappear through repetition.

Noel: There is another distinction. Changing valuation expectations do not necessarily mean the underlying business has deteriorated. Nor does extraordinary growth justify every proposed valuation. The filing describes the business. The market determines what investors are willing to pay for exposure to it. Those processes are related, not identical.

Noel: An investor considering Anthropic must weigh several realities. Revenue expanded from forty-one million dollars in twenty twenty-three to sixteen point two eight six billion dollars in the first half of twenty twenty-six. Most revenue comes from usage-based fees. A relatively small group of customers contributes meaningful sales. Nearly half of revenue during the first nine months of twenty twenty-six was routed through two major technology companies occupying multiple commercial and financial roles. Infrastructure spending is substantial, with commitments reaching decades ahead. And public investors would participate under governance arrangements preserving concentrated founder voting power and trust-controlled board authority. None of those facts supplies a definitive valuation. Together, they explain why valuation is judgment rather than a measurement taken directly from a filing. The numbers describe what Anthropic has built and committed to. They do not eliminate uncertainty.

Noel: Artificial intelligence has created businesses whose growth can be measured in extraordinary multiples. But rapid growth does not abolish ordinary financial questions. Who pays? How reliably do they pay? What does it cost to serve them? Who controls the company? And what obligations remain if expectations change? These questions do not dismiss Anthropic's achievements. They make those achievements financially intelligible. A company can be technologically remarkable, commercially successful, and difficult to value simultaneously.

Noel: The temptation is to reduce the story to enthusiasm against skepticism. One side sees extraordinary growth and imagines extraordinary value. Another sees enormous expenses and concentrated control. But the filing does not require a slogan. It requires careful reading. And a distinction between what happened and what might happen. Between what is disclosed and what is inferred. Between what a company earns and what investors might pay to own part of it. The reported valuation reset may prove significant. It may look different once an official price range appears and the offering proceeds. The information available does not establish that outcome. What it establishes is the architecture beneath the speculation. A business built on extraordinary consumption growth, dependent on expensive infrastructure, connected to powerful commercial partners, and governed through a structure limiting public shareholder influence. That architecture deserves attention regardless of the eventual offering price. Because a trillion-dollar headline tells us what people are discussing. It cannot tell us what they are buying.
